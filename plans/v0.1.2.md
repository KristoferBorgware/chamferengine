# v0.1.2

Patch: less at once. No item adds a capability. One item takes several away and
puts them behind a switch that gives them back.

Worked through in the three steps
[`HOW-TO-WRITE-PLANS.md`](../HOW-TO-WRITE-PLANS.md) sets out — discuss, try,
implement.

**The release is about being able to see one thing.** 0.1.0 built terrain, a
coarse map, rivers, water, caves, an atmosphere, a day, clouds, a moon and
stars, and 0.1.1 corrected four of them. Judging any one of those means looking
past the other nine, and the subsystem that is hardest to see is the one this
engine is actually about: cells, chunks and the level of detail between them.
Geometry is the only part of the picture that is not a color.

**Nothing is deleted.** A paused feature keeps its code, its tests and its
document. Pausing is a default, not a removal.

## Items

### I-1 — Everything is drawn at once, so no part of it can be judged

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-17 — 0.1.0 added too much at once, and what is
there now is a blend of everything rather than one thing that works
**Step:** 3, complete — candidate C

#### The problem

There is no way to look at the lattice. A frame carries scattered light, a sun
that moves, two cloud decks, a moon, a star field, water, rivers, continents,
snow lines, cliffs and cave mouths, and all of it is drawn over the geometry
that decides whether the engine is correct. A cell in the wrong place and a
cell in the right place under a cloud shadow look the same.

The level of detail is the worst affected. It is a change in triangle density
across a boundary, and every other feature in the frame changes the color on
both sides of that boundary for its own reasons.

#### What both candidates have to carry

The same list, which is the owner's:

| Paused | Becomes |
|---|---|
| Atmosphere | no scattering; one flat sky color |
| Day and night | no clock; the light does not move |
| Clouds | no deck built, no deck drawn |
| Moon | not drawn |
| Stars | not drawn |
| Coarse map | off — no continents, no rivers, no sea, no erosion |
| Noise | no detail term; the ground is a sphere |
| Water | none, at any depth |
| Light | constant, as at noon |

And the ground that is left is one material, green, with the occlusion the
mesher already bakes.

Three requirements sit under that list:

1. **A checkbox on the panel**, on by default, so the paused state is what a
   plain page load gives and unchecking it is what asks for the rest.
2. **Every knob a pause overrides is disabled**, not hidden. The panel already
   does this for the four knobs that follow the coarse map, through
   `enabledWhen`.
3. **Nothing is removed.** Every paused subsystem keeps its code and its tests.

#### The ground half needs no engine change, and this was measured

`coarseMap: false` already routes the generator through `flatCoarseMap`, whose
every field is zero. `TerrainGenerator`'s own formula then collapses without a
special case: `elevation = (0 − 0) × heightScale + detail`, and the water test
`coarseWaterRadius > coarseGroundRadius` can never be true. Setting
`detailAmplitude` to 0 removes the last term. Caves are already off by default.

Sampled over 6,500 columns spread across all 20 faces at those two settings:

| Reading | Result |
|---|---|
| Distinct ground radii | **1** |
| Columns with water | **0** |
| Surface block types | **grass, and nothing else** |

So the sphere is exact rather than nearly flat, the world is dry by
construction rather than by a sea level nobody reaches, and grass falls out of
the material rule on its own — elevation 0 is under the snow line and gradient
0 is under the cliff gradient. **Both halves of the ground list are two knob
values, and neither is a new code path.**

The sky half is the opposite: every one of the five paused sky features is
drawn by code that has no setting to turn it off.

#### Candidates

All three carry the same knob — `plain`, a boolean in `PlanetKnobs`, defaulting
to `true`. They differ on what that knob reaches.

**A — A paused feature is not drawn.** The knob is read at each site in the
frame loop that would draw one. The whole change is in `planet.ts` and
`PlanetSettings.ts`, and the diff is small. What it costs: the cloud worker is
still spawned and still builds two full decks that are then dropped, the sky
pipeline is still compiled, and the frame loop grows five branches.

**B — A paused feature is not built.** The knob is read once, at startup.
`SkyRenderer` is given a flag that makes it draw neither scatter nor stars nor
moon. What it costs: the flag has to reach `chamfer/render`, so the engine
learns what a pause is.

**C — Not built where it costs, not drawn where it does not.** Split by what
pausing actually saves. The cloud worker is never constructed, because that is
the real cost and it is client-side. The sky is left off the terrain pass
entirely rather than taught to draw nothing.

#### What separates them

Not how it looks — all three draw the same picture. They separate on what the
paused state actually is:

| | A | B | C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cloud worker | spawned, builds, discarded | never spawned | never spawned |
| Sky pipeline | compiled, drawn, overwritten | compiled, not drawn | never compiled |
| Branches in the frame loop | five | none | four, all null checks |
| Engine knows about the pause | no | yes | no |

**Chosen: C**, and building it turned up the reason it costs nothing that B
costs. `ChunkRenderer.layer` is already `PassLayer | null`, and the pass
already calls it as `this.layer?.before?.(...)`. So a sky that is not
constructed is a sky that is not drawn, with no flag and no new concept — and
because the sky's job is to fill every pixel at the far plane, leaving it off
lets the clear color the renderer is already given show through as one flat
sky. The atmosphere, the stars and the moon pause together, in one line, and
`chamfer/render` is untouched.

#### Built

`packages/client/src/PlanetSettings.ts`, `planet.ts`, `ParameterPanel.ts`.
**No engine file changed for this item.**

**The override is in two getters, not at the call sites.** `coarseMapRuns` and
`detailAmplitude` answer what a subsystem should do once the pause is applied,
and every reader inside `PlanetSettings` goes through them — `maxElevation`,
`groundSpan`, `tallestGroundOf`, `terrainOptions` and all four refusals in
`problems`. The stored knobs are never written to, so a person's coarse map and
detail settings are still there when they uncheck the box.

**The sky and the clouds are not constructed.** Under the pause `cloudSource`
and `sky` are both `null`: no worker is started, no deck is filled, no
scattering runs. The frame loop's four remaining branches are null checks on
`sky`, not questions about a mode.

**Noon is a local fact, and that is why the sun follows the player.** Walking
100 m turns a player's own up by 3.37°, so a sun fixed in world directions is a
lit hemisphere that can be walked out of rather than a constant. The pause
points the sun along the player's own up and holds daylight at 1, which puts
the sun overhead wherever they stand and leaves the terrain shader's night term
out of the mix entirely.

**The speckle stays.** `blockColor` moves each cell off its base green by up to
6% from the integer hash, and on a sphere with no relief it is the only thing
that makes one cell distinguishable from its neighbour — which is what the
release exists to look at. It is deterministic and it is not animated.

**The bench is explicitly un-paused.** It reads its world from
`PlanetSettings`, so the pause would have had it measuring a world nobody
plays. It now passes `plain: false` and `BENCH_plain=1` measures the paused one
instead. Its numbers are unchanged from what 0.1.1 recorded.

**Sixteen panel rows are disabled rather than hidden**, through the
`enabledWhen` the panel already had: the four that follow the coarse map now
also follow the pause, and twelve more gained a condition of their own. What
stays live is what the release is for — radius, block size, chunk, crust,
skirt, and the level-of-detail distance.

#### Trial

Judged in the engine, per
[`HOW-TO-WRITE-PLANS.md`](../HOW-TO-WRITE-PLANS.md): a plain planet is a
picture, and no script says whether it reads well. What could be measured
without a device was, and is a test rather than a one-off probe — at the
shipped defaults the ground is **one** distinct radius over 20 faces,
**0** wet columns and **grass alone** at the surface.

**F-011 binds.** The look itself is on real hardware.

### I-2 — The residency loop does not follow the player

**From:** F-001, F-002 and F-003, offered against I-1 and pulled in
**Step:** 3, complete — no candidates; the register already named the fix for
each, and there is one way to do each

#### The problem

Three bugs in one function, all of them distorting the subsystem I-1 exists to
expose:

- **Work is never called off.** `refresh` asks for every chunk in the new
  selection and never tells the pool about the ones that left it. Flying 200 m
  replaces 222 of 333 chunks, and every abandoned one is still generated,
  meshed and sent while the ground ahead waits behind it.
- **Arrivals are uploaded whether or not they are still wanted.** A mesh that
  finished after the player moved on costs a buffer and a draw until the next
  selection drops it.
- **The selection only runs while a direction key is held.** A player also
  moves without pressing anything: a fall off a ridge crosses several levels,
  and none of it was noticed until the next key press, which read as the world
  snapping resolution on landing.

#### Built

`packages/client/src/planet.ts`, the `refresh` function and the upload loop.

`building` became a `Map` from selection id to the selection itself, which is
what `source.cancel` needs. A chunk that leaves the selection is cancelled and
dropped from the map; the pool already drops a queued chunk outright and lets
one already on a worker run out. The `catch` that cleans up after a rejection
now checks that the request it is cleaning up after is still the outstanding
one, so a chunk cancelled and then asked for again is not deleted out from
under its second request.

The selection is held on the outer scope as `keep`, and an arrival whose key is
not in it is dropped rather than uploaded — which is still needed after the
cancellation fix, because a chunk already running is allowed to finish.

The trigger moved from `ahead !== 0 || aside !== 0 || lift !== 0` to distance
moved since the last selection, which covers falling, swimming and teleports in
one rule. The distance is two blocks: which level a chunk is drawn at changes
over tens of metres so this cannot step over a level change, and a selection
costs up to 4.9 ms at the worked settings, which is too much to spend every
frame.

### I-3 — The engine's own level-of-detail default is not the one that ships

**From:** F-009, offered against I-1 and pulled in
**Step:** 3, complete — no candidates; the register named the fix

#### The problem

`selectChunks` exported `DETAIL = 3` while every world built by the client used
2. The engine's figure was the default for a caller that passes nothing, and
nothing that ships passes nothing, so it was never used and never wrong enough
to notice. Anyone reading the engine to find out how detailed the world is got
the wrong answer.

#### Built

`packages/engine/src/generation/chunk/selectChunks.ts`. The default is 2 and
carries the measurement that chose it — chunks in view at 60 m of altitude,
which is the worst case because near and far chunks are both on screen there:
321 at 2, 471 at 2.5, 633 at 3. The client's copy of that reasoning came out,
since the number now lives with the default rather than beside it.

### I-4 — Standing on the plain sphere, the world is four chunks

**From:** the first look on real hardware, 2026-08-17 — the horizon ends in a
jagged mesh edge a few tens of metres out, and walking forward pops the next
chunk in
**Step:** 3, complete — three causes, each one measured, no candidates to
weigh: every fix is the formula the specification already writes down

#### The problem

The status line in the screenshot says it all: **4 of 4 chunks drawn, 4
held**, and **altitude −1.0 m** while standing on the surface. The selection
was four chunks on a planet of 5,120, with the mesh edge in plain view above
the sky line.

Reproduced exactly in the container with the same world — radius 850 m, 1 m
blocks, 48-cell chunks, plain: `selectChunks` at the shipped call returns
**3** (the count wobbles with where in a chunk the viewer stands).

#### Three causes, one chain

1. **The walkable surface was built one block below where the generator says
   it is.** `groundLayer` came from `layerOfRadius(groundRadius) + 1`, and a
   ground radius landing exactly on a layer boundary was assigned to the
   layer above the one it tops — a whole block of air where the ground should
   end, so the first solid block topped at `R − 1`. Noise terrain lands on an
   exact boundary almost never; the plain sphere lands on one at every
   column. The −1.0 m altitude readout is this bug on screen — and at 1 m
   blocks the ocean surface of every un-paused world sat a block under sea
   level for the same reason.

2. **The horizon was computed from the feet.** The client passed
   `player.position.length()` where `horizonAngle` wants the eye, and
   `horizonAngle` returns **0** for a viewer at or below the reference
   sphere. Feet at `R − 1` on a reference sphere of `R` is below it, so the
   horizon was zero and the only chunks kept were the ones the viewer's own
   direction touches. Cause 1 made this fire everywhere on the plain planet;
   on the un-paused world it fired anywhere the ground sits within an eye
   height of sea level — every shore.

3. **The selection had no peak term.** The specification's own worked
   constant: ground of height `h` is visible from
   `R·acos(R/(R+eye)) + R·acos(R/(R+h))` — a 60 m hill from 521 m. The
   selection stopped at the first term, so ground standing above the
   reference sphere beyond the geometric horizon was never selected and
   popped in late on every world, paused or not.

#### Built

`WorldShape.layerOfSurface(radius)` is the missing rounding: the first layer
whose top face is at or below the surface, `ceil` where `layerOfRadius` is
`floor`, with a billionth-of-a-layer tolerance because the arithmetic
rebuilding a boundary does not always land on it to the last bit — the 1700 m
test shape reproduces a boundary a last-bit high, and without the tolerance a
surface there skips a layer. `TerrainGenerator` derives `groundLayer` and
`waterLayer` through it; nothing else changes, because every consumer reads
blocks rather than assuming the offset.

`selectChunks` takes a `peakHeight` and reaches
`horizonAngle(viewerRadius, surfaceRadius) + horizonAngle(surfaceRadius +
peakHeight, surfaceRadius)`. The client passes the eye and
`shape.maxElevation`; the bench passes the same at its three sites.

Measured on the screenshot's world, standing on the surface:

| | Shipped | Fixed |
|---|---|---|
| Surface top − R | −1.000 m | **0.000 m** |
| Chunks selected | 3–4 | **25** |
| Selection reaches | underfoot | **93.3 m** |
| Visual horizon | 52.1 m | 52.1 m |

The mesh edge now sits 41 m past what curvature lets the eye see, so there is
no rim to look at and nothing pops: reselection runs every two metres of
movement and new chunks enter beyond visibility.

**What the peak term costs, honestly.** The un-paused world's `maxElevation`
is ~120 m, whose own horizon is ~1.3 km, and the bench moved accordingly:
eye-height flat ground went from 286 to 552 chunks and 2.4 to 3.9 s to build
a view. That is the ground the old selection was wrongly dropping — mountains
on screen late — but most of the new ring holds no peak, so much of it is
cost without a picture. The refinement — a per-chunk height bound instead of
one planet-wide worst case — is F-023, deferred rather than designed here.
The plain planet's `maxElevation` is 1 m and its ring is three metres wide.

Register searched for the item: F-006 touches the same subsystem
(`residentChunks`, written and unused) and is offered, not pulled in —
nothing here made it worse or better.

### I-5 — Level joins show seams: holes to the sky and a step per level

**From:** the second look on real hardware, 2026-08-17 — jagged sky-colored
lines along chunk boundaries from orbit, and a dotted line across the plain
sphere; the owner asked for "some sort of merging between patches"
**Step:** 3, complete — two mechanisms, each measured

#### The problem

Two separate defects meet at every boundary between chunks drawn at different
levels, and each was measured on the screenshots' own worlds:

1. **Holes.** A chunk draws the cells whose centres fall inside its triangle,
   at its own level's lattice. Two levels tile their shared boundary with
   hexagons of two different sizes, and those do not interlock: strips of
   ground have their containing cell centred across the line, at a lattice the
   chunk over there does not use, so nobody's own cells draw them and the sky
   shows through the planet. Sampled at the second screenshot's altitude on
   its world: **0.18% of the covered sky is drawn by nobody**, in a dotted
   line along every level join — and at orbit's level-7 chunks the same gaps
   are up to half a 128 m cell wide, which is the first screenshot's jagged
   blue.

2. **A step per level.** Each level rounded its surfaces to its own layer
   grid. On the plain planet the surface sat at R, R−1, R−3 and R−7 at levels
   0 through 3 — a whole coarser block down at each ring — and at 1 m blocks
   the un-paused ocean surface did the same, so every join was a cliff even
   where the terrain function put both sides at an identical radius.

#### What "merging" can mean here

True vertex welding is impossible: Goldberg levels do not nest, so a coarse
hexagon is never a union of fine ones and the two boundaries share no
vertices to weld. What is possible is making the levels **agree wherever the
terrain agrees**, and connecting them with terrain-colored walls where it
does not. That is what was built.

#### Built

**Every level's surface caps snap to one shared grid** — the finest level's,
which is the world's real block grid. `Column` now carries the terrain
function's own `groundRadius` and `waterRadius` past the rounding, and the
mesher (`surfaceGrid` in `MeshOptions`) quantizes the ground and water caps,
the tops of the walls that reach them, and the skirts that hang from them, to
that grid instead of the chunk's own. The grid hangs from the crust top,
which every level shares, so two levels snapping one radius get one answer:
on a flat world every level's whole surface lands at the same radius — the
test asserts fine and coarse agree within two centimetres where they used to
differ by a full coarse block — and the un-paused ocean is one surface at
every level. Steps now exist only where the terrain sampled at two spacings
genuinely differs, and there they are real information. Caps deeper down —
cave ceilings and floors — stay on the chunk's own grid, where nothing joins
them.

**An apron closes the tiling gap.** With the skirt on, each chunk also draws
the ring of cells just beyond its rim — its own lattice, one cell deep — a
centimetre below their true height (`APRON_DROP`). Where the neighbour is at
the same level it draws those very cells itself and the apron sits invisibly
underneath; where the levels differ, the apron is what shows in the strips
neither side's own cells cover. A centimetre is chosen against `float32`:
the same polygon written against two chunk origins lands on two roundings,
and two coincident copies would sparkle. The reach is right by construction —
each side patches its own triangle at its own scale — and the test samples
6,000 directions across a mixed-level selection: some ground is nobody's own
cell, and **none of it escapes the apron**.

**What it costs.** The apron is a rim ring of surface caps and skirts plus
one more ring of column samples: the bench's eye-height scenes grew from
1.93M to 2.33M triangles (+20%) and a view fill from 3.9 to 4.6 s (+16%).
The `Skirt` knob switches the apron with the skirt, and its description now
says so — zero shows the cracks and the holes.

**The third look found the two tails of the same item.** A sparse dotted
line survived on the plain planet, and on relief the joins opened wide along
terrace brinks:

- **A face's coordinates were trusted across faces.** Both `outward` and the
  apron's ownership guard tested a neighbour's `(i, j)` against this chunk's
  triangle without checking whose face the numbers belong to, and a foreign
  cell's coordinates can path-match by coincidence. Level joins follow the
  face hierarchy, so the boundaries most in need of the apron were exactly
  where it quietly skipped cells — and where some rim skirts had been
  missing since 0.1.0. A test now computes the apron set from the addressing
  alone and holds `tally.apron` to it, on face-edge chunks included; it was
  red before the fix on exactly those.
- **A fixed skirt cannot reach a cliff.** Two levels put a cliff's edge at
  horizontally different places, so their surfaces disagree by the whole
  cliff height where a join crosses one, and two cells of skirt covered two.
  Doc 14's full answer is seam-owned faces, which needs the neighbour's
  level; the neighbour-blind half taken instead: `seamFloor` evaluates the
  terrain one level finer and one coarser at each rim column — the bracket a
  neighbour can be — and the skirt reaches the lowest surface either would
  put there. Walls only ever hang downward from a chunk's own surface, so
  against a same-level neighbour the extra reach is buried and nothing
  stands into the air. Costs two column evaluations per rim and apron cell:
  the bench's view fill went from 4.6 to 6.5 s, triangle counts unchanged.
  What this does not close is a cave mouth at a join — F-025.

**The fourth look found what the merge itself had opened.** With the seams
closed, thin slits appeared along terrace brinks at coarse levels — walls
missing, caps intact. The merge is why: two neighbours level at a chunk's
own coarse grid can stand several fine layers apart once their caps snap to
the shared fine grid, and the side runs never covered that span, because at
the chunk's own resolution the two columns are the same height and no run
exists there at all. Before the merge their caps were flush and no wall was
needed; after it, the step was real and bare. The mesher now emits a cap
step — the wall between the two snapped caps — wherever a solid neighbour's
cap snapped lower, in owned cells and apron cells alike. The test finds
level pairs with differing snapped caps in a coarse chunk and demands a
triangle spanning both radii at their shared edge, which only a wall can
be; it is red with the step walls disabled. Cost on the bench: the relief
scene went from 2.37M to 3.16M triangles — terrace brinks are long, and
every brink is now walled at fine resolution.

Register searched for the item: F-005 (nothing checks the renderer produces
a picture) is why both defects lived to be seen by eye on hardware rather
than by any gate; it stays open, large as ever. Noticed and recorded on the
way: F-024 and F-025.

### I-6 — A level switch drops the ground before its replacement exists

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-17 — "the current lod step is thrown out before
we had the ability to generate the next lod step. This means that we are
constantly seeing holes in the planet when we switch lod step."
**Step:** 3, complete — two causes, one of them invisible on every world this
container had tried

#### The problem

The residency loop dropped a chunk the moment it left the selection, while
its replacement at the other level was still queued on a worker: a real hole
straight through the planet, at every level-ring transition, for as long as a
chunk takes to build. The holes were transient, which is why every
watertightness probe of the *meshes* came back clean — the geometry was
whole; the lifecycle was not. Raycast probes across full mixed-level scenes
confirmed the static picture is watertight inside the reach a player can see.

Underneath it sat a second, silent defect: `selectionId` packed the chunk key
into a `2^20` field, and a key is not small — 20 triangles split `level`
times is `20 * 4^level`, past five million at chunk level 9, which is exactly
a 16-cell chunk on a depth-13 world. Colliding ids share one slot in every
resident set — `drawn`, `building`, the renderer's residency — so each upload
evicted the other and ground flickered with holes no mesh ever had. None of
this container's fixture worlds go past chunk level 4, which is why no probe
here could reproduce what the owner saw daily.

#### Built

`selectionId` packs the key into a `2^40` field — exact in `float64` to the
deepest world the address word allows — and `selectionOf` reads it back. A
test walks the scales and pins the depth-13 counts that collided.

A chunk leaving the selection now **retires** instead of dropping:
`chunkOverlaps` says which wanted chunks cover its triangle — ancestors and
descendants, one path a prefix of the other on one face — and the chunk keeps
drawing until every one of them is uploaded. The sweep runs after each
selection and after each upload, so the ground under a retiring chunk is
never bare. The cost is brief overdraw where old and new levels coexist,
which the merge makes coincident rather than conflicting.

**The seam overlay, asked for by the owner, ships on the panel.** `Seam
overlay` rebuilds the world with the joins painted instead of hidden: yellow
cells sit on a face edge, blue cells on a chunk boundary, orange is the apron
ring a chunk draws past its own rim. It is the instrument for attributing any
remaining artifact to the boundary type it sits on, and it cost one flag
through the worker setup and a tint at the mesher's eight color sites.

Alongside, two robustness holes in I-5's apron closed: the three triangle
corners now apron the corner cell and its full ring outright, because a cell
clipping the corner wedge can sit two steps from every owned cell; and
`outward` no longer trusts the rim rows alone, because a boundary row can
belong wholly to the neighbouring chunk and the canonical-face rule refuses
cells inside the triangle on a face edge — either way the true boundary of
the drawn set sits one row in from where the shortcut looked.

**The stacked price of blind seams, stated once.** The relief bench scene has
gone from 1.53M triangles before I-5 to 3.78M after all of its passes and
this item's — merged caps, aprons, corner rings, step walls, deepened skirts,
boundary-edge skirts. Each pass closed a real hole and none of them knows
what the neighbouring chunk actually draws. F-025's neighbour-aware meshing
is the road that gets the triangles back, and it is deliberately not taken
inside a patch release.

## Noticed and not fixed

**F-021 — daylight is measured at the spawn point.** `ground` is assigned while
the world is being set up and never again, so `daylight()` reports how lit the
spawn point is however far the player walks. Found while holding the light at
noon for I-1. It is not fixed here because the day is one of the paused
features and judging a fix would mean turning it back on, and it is not
visible here for the same reason.

**F-022 — the chase camera does not drive the selection.** The wheel puts the
camera up to 60 m up and behind, the selection still reads the player's eye,
and zooming does not trigger a reselect at all. Found fixing I-4, where the
question of which radius the horizon belongs to was the whole item.

**F-023 — the peak term assumes the tallest ground is everywhere.** One
planet-wide `maxElevation` reaches every peak and pays for it in a ring that
is mostly empty: 286 to 552 chunks at eye height on the un-paused world. A
per-chunk height bound, which the coarse map could supply, would shrink it.
Found pricing I-4's fix on the bench.

**F-024 — the column cache key collides at depth 17.** `ChunkColumnSampler`
packs `(face, i, j)` with 65,536 multipliers and the lattice reaches 131,072
at the deepest world the address word allows. Found writing I-5's apron,
whose own key packs with 262,144 for exactly this reason.
