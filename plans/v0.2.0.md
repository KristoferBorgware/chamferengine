# v0.2.0

Minor: the coarse map becomes a stack of maps, and gains an editor to judge it
by. Items may add capability.

Worked through in the three steps
[`HOW-TO-WRITE-PLANS.md`](../HOW-TO-WRITE-PLANS.md) sets out — discuss, try,
implement. Steps 1 and 2 run per item; step 3 runs once, for the whole release,
and starts when every item in the scope has cleared step 2.

**The release is about being able to tune what the planet is made of.** 0.1.2
made one subsystem visible by pausing nine others. This release takes the
subsystem that was paused hardest — the coarse map — and gives it a coastline
worth looking at, the fields a world needs beyond height, and a surface on
which a person can change a number and see the result before a world is built
from it.

**The instrument comes first.** Changing a map knob today reloads the page: a
new device, a level-8 map, and every chunk meshed again. Six of the nine items
below are chosen by looking, and none of them can be judged through that loop.
I-5 is therefore built before the items it is used to settle.

**The editor rebuilds the maps at full resolution and leaves the terrain
alone.** Apply is what takes the maps to the terrain. A map at the shipped
level is `3.4 s` of work against a whole world rebuilt, so the maps are never
drawn at a reduced level and a preview is the map by construction.

**Every existing seed produces a different world.** I-1 changes where the land
is. Nothing is saved yet, so nothing is lost.

### I-1 — The coastline is a threshold on noise, so the land comes out as blobs

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18 — reference images from a world editor whose
coastlines are ragged and strewn with islands, against ours which are not
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

`continentHeight` sums two tiers of `fbm` and `seaLevelFor` cuts the result at
the percentile that leaves the intended land fraction standing. A percentile
cut through a smooth field draws a smooth curve. The coasts come out rounded,
islands are rare and roughly circular, and there is no archipelago anywhere on
the planet.

The measurements that describe this do not exist yet. What a trial has to
produce, for each candidate and for what ships today:

- **Coastline perimeter over the square root of land area**, which is how
  ragged a coast is, independent of how much land there is.
- **Island count and their size distribution**, an island being a connected run
  of land cells not touching the largest landmass.
- **Longest river on the largest landmass.** Doc 21 already measures the
  continent tier this way — 31 cells on small blobs, 86 on a large landmass —
  so the number is comparable against what is there now.

#### Candidates

**A — Warp the direction before the continent lookup.** Sample a second `fbm`
field and use it to displace the unit direction, then evaluate the continent
tier at the displaced point. A smooth field pushed around by another smooth
field has a folded, filamented level set, so the same percentile cut draws a
ragged curve. Two new parameters: how far the warp pushes, and at what feature
size.

What it keeps: the map stays a pure function of a direction, evaluable at any
point at any resolution, with nothing stored and no build stage added. What it
costs: one more `fbm` evaluation per cell, and the coastline's character is
still a by-product of a threshold rather than something set directly.

**B — Grow the land level by level on the lattice.** Hold a binary land or sea
state per cell and build it up the subdivision hierarchy. At the coarsest level
every cell is land with a hashed probability (**creation**), and a few isolated
cells are seeded away from the rest (**island**). Refining to the next level,
each child inherits its parent's state and then flips with a probability that
depends on how many of its neighbours hold the other state (**growth weight**
and **growth factor**). Repeat to the map's level.

Randomness is hashed from the seed and the cell's lattice coordinates, never
drawn from a running generator, so the result does not depend on the order
cells are visited and a level can be built without building the ones beside it.

What it keeps: the coastline is set directly, at a resolution per level. What
it costs: a stored mask, a build stage, and a field that cannot be evaluated
below the level it was built at.

**C — Put the land on plates.** Scatter a few dozen seeds and give every cell
its nearest, which is a Voronoi diagram on the sphere and the same `argmax` of
dot products doc 04 uses for faces. Give each plate an angular velocity — a
hashed axis and rate, so a plate's motion at a cell is the cross product of that
axis with the cell — and a hashed bias that makes it oceanic or continental.
Where two plates close on each other along the step between two cells, raise a
range; where they part, drop a rift. Carry that inland, weaker at every step.
Height is the bias plus the range plus relief, and sea level is still a
percentile of it.

Every direction is hashed and normalised rather than built from an angle. A
plate laid out with `sin` and `cos` would put a transcendental in a field two
clients have to agree on to the bit; three hashed components and one
`normalize` are a wrapping multiply and a square root, both pinned by IEEE 754.

Doc 21 has recommended this since before the engine existed, in **Continents,
and where they come from**. It is the only one of the four that puts a mountain
range somewhere for a reason.

**Conway's rule itself is not a candidate.** Its birth and survival counts are
written for an eight-neighbour square neighbourhood; on six neighbours, five at
the twelve pentagons, every threshold has to be re-derived. What the reference
images show is the growth-and-refine loop rather than the rule, and B carries
that without inheriting a numbering that does not fit the grid.

#### What decides it

Beyond the three measurements above, one property may settle it on its own.
**A hierarchical process makes a preview an exact prefix of the result.** I-5
previews at a lower level than it applies at. Candidate B built to level 6 is
the first six levels of candidate B built to level 8, so the preview coastline
does not move when Apply is pressed. Candidate A's sea level is a percentile of
the field as sampled, so it shifts with resolution and the preview coastline
moves.

If that holds, the trial has to say by how much: the fraction of cells whose
land or sea state differs between a level-6 preview and the level-8 result, for
each candidate.

**Refuted by the trial below, and in the other direction.** The paragraph above
is kept as it was written, because what was expected and what was measured are
both worth having.

### Trial

`verification/coastline.js`. One seed, land fraction 0.3, levels 5 to 8.

**How ragged, as a rate rather than a shape.** Count the cell edges along the
largest landmass's coast, halve the cells, count again. A curve carrying no
detail below the map's resolution doubles exactly. The excess over 2 is what
ragged means as a number, and it does not depend on which level it is read at.

| | level 5 | 6 | 7 | growth | dimension |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| today | 781 | 1,623 | 3,523 | `x2.08` `x2.17` | **1.06 – 1.12** |
| warped | 827 | 1,865 | 4,087 | `x2.26` `x2.19` | **1.13 – 1.17** |
| grown | 1,007 | 2,655 | 7,239 | `x2.64` `x2.73` | **1.40 – 1.45** |
| plates | 993 | 2,807 | 6,373 | `x2.83` `x2.27` | **1.18 – 1.50** |

Real coasts, quoted and not measured here, run from about 1.05 for South Africa
through 1.25 for Britain to about 1.52 for Norway. **What ships is at the smooth
end of the real range rather than outside it**, which is a milder verdict than
the item assumed.

**What each does to the land**, at level 7:

| | land | largest landmass | islands | longest river |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| today | 30.0% | 27,305 | 45 | **172** |
| warped | 30.0% | 26,913 | 43 | 153 |
| grown | 30.4% | 14,910 | 312 | 83 |
| plates | 30.0% | 25,072 | 136 | 94 |

**The two that build structure both charge for it, in different places.**
Growing the mask gives up the land fraction as a number that can be asked for —
there is no percentile in it, so `creation` was searched by bisection and
arrives near 30% rather than on it — and halves the largest landmass. Plates
keep both, and lose the river length a different way: a range along every seam
cuts the interior into separate basins, so a river runs from a ridge to the
nearest coast instead of across the continent. The land is not what limits it
there; the ground is.

**The preview prediction was wrong, and backwards.**

| | cells of the level-6 map the level-8 map disagrees with, of 40,962 |
|---|---|
| today | 7 — `0.017%` |
| warped | 4 — `0.010%` |
| grown | 901 — `2.200%` |
| plates | 1,528 — `3.730%` |

Noise is sampled from a direction, so a cell that exists at both levels is
handed the same height at both, and only the percentile moves. **Only the two
noise fields preview faithfully.** A field built by running a process over the
grid disagrees with itself across levels, because the grid is what the process
runs on: the grown mask reconsiders inherited cells at every level, and a plate
range is a band a fixed number of cells wide, which cannot be narrower than one
cell on the coarser map however few metres it is meant to be.

**This constrains nothing, because I-5 previews at full resolution.** The
numbers above answer a question the release turned out not to ask: the editor
rebuilds the map at the level the world will use, so a preview is the map by
construction and every candidate here is equal on that axis. What the table
still says is what happens when somebody changes the coarse spacing — a noise
field keeps its coastline and the other two redraw it.

**Warping is close to a no-op at the one setting tried** — dimension 1.13 to
1.17 against 1.06 to 1.12, which is not a change anyone would see. Only `0.35`
amplitude at frequency `1.6` was run, so the candidate is not finished; a sweep
of the amplitude is what is left.

**What the trial got wrong on the way.** The plate uplift first spread the seam
value from the cell it was reached from, which is a seam cell only on the first
step and carries zero afterwards, so every range came out two cells wide
whatever the range was set to. That made the ranges a level-dependent width in
metres, and it read as a property of plates until it was measured: the
disagreement above was 5.80% before the fix and 3.73% after.

### I-2 — Nothing knows how far it is from the sea

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18 — the banded map in the reference images
**Step:** 1

#### The goal

Give every cell one number: **how many metres to the nearest coast**, negative
at sea and positive on land.

Three things read that one number, which is why it is a field and not three
separate calculations.

- **The baseline height.** Ground that falls away as it leaves the shore and
  rises as it goes inland, before any noise is added. Today there is no such
  tier — the ground goes straight from summed noise to a sea level, so a coast
  has no shelf and an interior has no rise.
- **Humidity**, in I-6. How far you are from water is most of what humidity is.
- **Coastal materials**, later. A beach, a shelf and a salt flat are each a
  band at a distance.

The banded map in the reference images is this field drawn with a repeating
palette, which is why its contours follow every inlet.

#### Candidates

**A — Count rings.** Start at every cell that touches a cell of the other kind.
Step outward one ring at a time, writing 1, 2, 3, and so on. Multiply by the
cell spacing at the end to get metres.

**B — Add up real distances.** Start in the same places, but each step adds the
actual metres between the two cells, taken off a sorted queue. `MinHeap` is
already here for `fillPits`.

The difference is that **cells are not all the same size**: spacing varies
`1.41:1` across the sphere. So counting rings measures a slightly different
distance in different parts of the planet, and A's bands may come out wider at
a face centre than at a face corner. **The trial is whether that shows** once
the profile below is drawn on it. A is one pass and no queue; B cannot band
unevenly.

#### The profile

The baseline height is a lookup. Hand it a distance, it hands back metres above
or below sea level. Two ways to write that lookup.

**A — Named points, straight lines between them.** "At the coast, 0 m. Four
hundred metres out to sea, −60 m. Three kilometres inland, +80 m." Every number
is a place a person can find on the map and point at.

**B — One curve, two numbers.** How high the interior gets, and how quickly it
gets there. Fewer numbers, and neither of them is anywhere in particular.

**A is the one to try first.** This release exists because there are knobs
whose effect cannot be seen. A breakpoint stated in metres is a knob whose
effect can be seen, and there being four of them rather than two is not what
makes a panel hard to use.

### Trial, so far

Ring counting is built, in `verification/coastline.js` section 3, and it
**reaches every cell**: on the level-7 grown mask the field runs from 47 cells
at the deepest sea to 26 cells inland, with none left unvisited. One pass, no
queue.

The profile was run with breakpoints at `-60`, `-6`, `-1`, `1`, `40` and `200`
cells, and it does something the item did not predict. **It tempers the coast
rather than inheriting it.** Laying relief over the baseline and re-cutting at
sea level pulls the thinnest filaments back under: the grown mask's ratio falls
from `36.78` to `28.61` and its islands from 312 to 193.

That matters for I-1 as well as here — the coastline a player sees is the one
after the profile, not the mask.

**What is left**: whether counting rings bands unevenly, which needs the two
distance fields drawn side by side rather than counted, and is what the editor
is for.

### I-3 — The map is four fields wide and one width deep

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18 — memory is barely used, and the landscape is
poorer for it
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

`CoarseMap` holds `height`, `water`, `flow` and `slope`, all `Float32Array`,
all named as properties, and `toSnapshot` lists them one by one. This release
adds at least six more: the land mask, border distance, baseline height,
temperature, humidity, biome noise and biome.

Two things are wrong with growing it as it stands. **Every field costs four
bytes whether or not it needs them** — a biome is a byte, a mask is a bit, a
step count is two bytes. And **every consumer names every field**, so the
snapshot, the worker protocol and the editor's list of what it can draw all
have to be edited together each time one is added.

The arithmetic, at level 8's 655,362 cells:

| Field | Width | Bytes |
|---|---|---|
| height, water, flow, slope, baseline | `float32` | 2.5 MB each |
| border distance | `int16` | 1.3 MB |
| land mask | `uint8` | 0.65 MB |
| temperature, humidity, biome noise, biome | `uint8` | 0.65 MB each |

Eleven fields at their own widths is about 16 MB. The same eleven at
`float32` is 27 MB. The difference is one more level of map, which is what
actually buys landscape.

#### Candidates

**A — A described set.** A field is a name, a width, a range and a way to color
it, held in a table; the map holds the arrays against those descriptions. The
editor draws a field it is handed without knowing which one it is, and adding a
map is one entry. What it costs: a lookup by name where there is a property
today, and the compiler stops checking that a reader asked for a field that
exists.

**B — Named properties, each at its own width.** The map keeps a property per
field and gains the new ones as properties. Types hold end to end. What it
costs: the editor carries a hand-written list of what it can draw, and adding a
map edits four places.

**The measurement is bytes and copy time**: total resident bytes at levels 8
and 9, and how long `toSnapshot` takes to reach a worker, for each candidate at
the full eleven fields.

### I-4 — The detail term does not know how coarsely it will be drawn

**From:** reading the generator while scoping this release, 2026-08-18
**Findings pulled in:** F-023
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

`TerrainGenerator.columnAt(face, i, j)` takes no level. `lod` exists in
`selectChunks.ts` and stops there. So a chunk drawn two levels coarse evaluates
the same detail field at the same frequency, with its cells four times further
apart, and keeps whatever the field happened to hold at those points.

That is sampling a signal below its own rate. The surface a coarse chunk draws
is not a smoothed version of the fine one — it is a different, arbitrary
selection from it, so the two disagree by the full detail amplitude at a join
and the disagreement changes as a chunk switches level.

Nothing has measured it. What the trial has to produce: the height difference
between one column evaluated at the finest level and at each coarser one, over
a large sample, and how much of the step at a level join it accounts for.

**F-023 belongs here.** Its stated fix is a height bound per chunk rather than
one planet-wide `maxElevation`, and it names the coarse map as the thing that
already knows the height field. A pyramid of tallest and lowest ground per
triangle, at the chunk levels that matter, is tens of kilobytes and answers
both questions.

#### The coarse map has the same problem, three LOD steps deep

A chunk drawn at LOD `N` spaces its cells `2^N` blocks apart and reads the
coarse map at that spacing. Reading a map more widely than its own cell is the
same undersampling, so at the shipped defaults — 1 m blocks, a 32 m coarse
cell, depth 13, chunk level 8:

| LOD | cell spacing | reads the 32 m map |
|---|---|---|
| 0 – 5 | 1 m – 32 m | inside the map's own resolution |
| 6 | 64 m | undersampled |
| 7 | 128 m | undersampled |
| 8 | 256 m | undersampled |

**Three of the nine LOD steps, and the three furthest away.** The fix would be
a pyramid: average each field down a level at a time and let a coarse chunk read
the level that matches its spacing. Lattice points nest exactly, so a coarse
point is a fine point and the average is over the ring around it. It costs a
third more memory, because a pyramid is `1 + 1/4 + 1/16` of the map.

**The detail term is five steps worse and comes first.** It undersamples from
LOD 1, so it is the term to band-limit and measure before any pyramid is built.
Whether the coarse map still shows anything at LOD 6 once the detail term is
fixed is a measurement nobody has, and it decides whether the pyramid is worth
its third.

#### Candidates

**A — Drop octaves.** Pass the level to `columnAt` and stop the `fbm` loop at
the octave whose feature size falls below two cells at that level. The count is
an integer, so a chunk changing level changes octave count in one step and the
surface jumps by that octave's amplitude.

**B — Roll the amplitudes off.** Keep every octave and scale each one against
the cell spacing at that level, so an octave fades out as it approaches the
sampling limit rather than vanishing. The surface moves continuously as a chunk
changes level, and the noise evaluations are not saved.

The trade is stated in the two sentences above: A saves the evaluations and
steps; B is continuous and saves nothing. **The measurement is how far the
surface moves at a level change**, and against it, the evaluations each
candidate leaves in a chunk build.

### Trial, in progress

`verification/lod.js`. The shipped detail term — 5 m over 112 m features, four
octaves — against the average of the ground each cell covers, at 190 places on
one face.

**What the octaves can carry.** An octave needs two cells across a feature. The
four run at 112, 56, 28 and 14 m and carry 2.67, 1.33, 0.67 and 0.33 m of the
5 m. So the finest is unrepresentable past LOD 2 and **the whole term is gone by
LOD 6**, which is exactly where the coarse map starts being read more widely
than its own cell.

**How far the drawn ground is from the honest answer**, in metres:

| LOD | today | drop octaves | roll off |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.02 | 0.02 | 0.02 |
| 3 | 0.02 | 0.12 | 0.03 |
| 5 | 0.15 | 0.53 | 0.25 |
| 6 | 0.31 | 1.02 | 0.50 |

**Both candidates come out worse than doing nothing, and that is not yet
understood.** Three things could produce it and they have not been separated.
The engine's `fbm` divides by the summed amplitude of the octaves it ran, so
asking it for fewer octaves rescales what is left rather than removing content —
band-limiting wants the removal, not the rescale, and the trial may have
measured one while describing the other. The reference may be wrong: a footprint
averaged over a square of lattice steps is not the hexagon a cell actually
covers. And an octave's average over a footprint is not zero, so removing it
whole is not the same as removing what the cell cannot see.

**Nothing is chosen here yet.** What the section does establish is the size of
the problem — `0.31 m` at LOD 6 on 1 m blocks, growing with the level — and that
it is bounded by the detail amplitude rather than by the terrain.

**What is left**: separate the three causes above, then measure the coarse map's
own contribution at LOD 6 to 8, which is what decides whether the pyramid in the
section above earns its third of the map's memory.

### I-5 — Tuning a map costs a page reload

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18 — the knobs are hard to form a judgement about,
and there are too many of them
**Findings pulled in:** F-027
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

`ParameterPanel.rebuild()` sets `location.href`. Changing one number reloads
the document, asks for a GPU device, builds a coarse map at the world's own
level, and meshes every chunk in view. Nothing about that loop is wrong for
building a world, and all of it is in the way of choosing a number.

Six items in this release are settled by looking at a map. None of them can be
settled through a loop that costs a reload each time.

**What the panel is today**: 26 knobs, 24 rows, 7 groups, one column beside a
playable world, behind `?panel=1`.

**What it needs to become**: `?panel=1` turns on editor mode, and editor mode
carries panes that show and hide independently — the world knobs, the map
editor, the readouts. A pane is shown because it is being used, not because
the mode is on.

#### The map editor

A pane holding the map stack: a list of fields, one drawn at a time, on a flat
equirectangular canvas beside the existing sphere. Changing a map knob rebuilds
the map and redraws it, and nothing else happens — no device, no mesher, no
chunk. **Apply** builds the world.

Equirectangular is the drawing, never the storage. It is a projection of the
lattice for a person to look at, and it stretches at the poles, which is a
property of the picture rather than of the planet.

#### What one map costs

Measured at the shipped defaults, with the grid kept between runs:

| stage | level 7 | level 8 | level 9 |
|---|---|---|---|
| grid — no knob changes it | 171 ms | 413 ms | 1,642 ms |
| height field | 353 ms | **1,361 ms** | 5,268 ms |
| sea level | 18 ms | 79 ms | 348 ms |
| erosion | 355 ms | **1,585 ms** | 8,031 ms |
| flow | 78 ms | 391 ms | 1,841 ms |
| slope | 11 ms | 34 ms | 137 ms |
| **knob to map** | **815 ms** | **3,450 ms** | **15,625 ms** |

A timing, so it moves run to run; read the shares rather than the values. Two
things decide the shape of this item. **The grid is not rebuilt** — nothing but
the coarse spacing changes it, so it is built once and kept. And **erosion and
the height field are 85% of what is left**, which is where both candidates
below aim.

Against this, a world rebuild is the whole terrain: every chunk generated,
meshed and uploaded. The map is the fast half already.

#### Candidates

**A — Re-run only what the knob reaches.** The stages form a chain, and most
knobs do not enter it at the top. Land only reaches sea level and below, so it
skips the height field. Erosion rate reaches erosion and below, so it skips the
height field and sea level. Hold each stage's output and recompute from the
first one the knob touches.

What it gives: the knobs that skip the height field come back in about `2.0 s`
instead of `3.4 s`. What it does not give: erosion is downstream of almost
everything, so almost every knob still pays for it.

**B — Show it before it is finished.** Run the whole chain on a worker and paint
each stage as it lands — the raw height field first, then the coastline once
sea level is known, then the eroded surface, then the rivers. The first useful
picture is on screen after the height field rather than after all of it.

What it gives: every knob feels the same, and the one a person is turning is
usually visible in the first stage. What it costs: cancellation, because a knob
dragged through ten values queues ten runs, which is the shape of F-001's fix on
the residency loop.

**The measurement is two times, not one**: how long until there is a picture
worth looking at, and how long until it is the final map — for a knob at the top
of the chain and a knob near the bottom.

#### F-027 is what lets any of this be checked here

[`HOW-TO-TAKE-A-FRAME.md`](../HOW-TO-TAKE-A-FRAME.md) carries the four Chromium
flags, the protocol calls and the way to read the pixels back, so the client
runs on a software adapter here and hands a real frame back. What F-027 has
left is the harness itself: about eighty lines in `tools/`, beside the other
checks a session runs before a push.

Every item in this release that is judged by looking goes through it, so it is
built with the editor rather than after it. It settles what is drawn and never
how fast — the frames are a software rasteriser's.

### I-6 — A planet has height and nothing else about it

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18
**Step:** 1

#### The goal

Four more fields, generated, documented, verified and demonstrated, and read by
nothing:

| Field | Holds |
|---|---|
| temperature | how warm a cell is |
| humidity | how wet |
| biome noise | a decorrelating field, so biome edges are not perfectly zonal |
| biome | one index per cell, from the other three and the elevation |

**Nothing consumes them in this release.** They are built so the model can be
argued about against a picture rather than in the abstract, and so the field
stack has more than one shape of field in it before I-3's decision is locked.

The axis is not a choice: doc 20 fixes the poles at icosahedron vertices 0 and
3 and the prime meridian at vertex 11, so latitude is already defined and every
pentagon sits at a multiple of 36° of longitude.

#### Bands at real latitudes are a requirement, not a candidate

The dry places go where they go on a real planet: wet at the equator, dry at
the horse latitudes, wet again in the temperate band, dry at the poles. A model
that scatters deserts wherever its noise happens to fall does not meet that,
so it is not a candidate — it is a model that fails the requirement.

Temperature is settled by the same sentence. It falls from equator to pole and
falls again with elevation at a stated lapse rate, plus a little noise. Three
numbers a person can picture: equator temperature, pole temperature, lapse
rate. Both candidates below share it and neither changes it.

What is left open is **how humidity gets its bands**, and the two ways differ
in what they give beyond the bands.

#### Candidates

**A — Read humidity off the latitude.** A curve of humidity against latitude
with the four bands written into it, plus I-2's distance to water, plus noise.
One lookup per cell. Every planet gets the same bands in the same places, and
a desert is a latitude.

**B — Carry the moisture.** Start with moisture over water, move it along doc
32's single wind axis for a fixed number of steps, and drop it where the ground
rises. The bands still appear, because evaporation and the wind both follow
latitude — but they arrive as a result rather than as a table, and two things
come with them that A cannot produce: **a dry shadow behind a mountain range**,
and **a continental interior that dries with distance from the coast**.

What it costs: an iterative pass over the grid rather than a lookup, a number
of steps nobody can set by looking at one cell, and it makes doc 32's wind axis
a climate input when it was pinned for clouds.

#### What decides it

Both have to band, so banding is the gate rather than the measurement: the
latitude distribution of the driest cells, which should show four bands per
hemisphere either way.

The measurement that separates them is **whether a desert is ever behind a
mountain**. Count dry cells downwind of high ground against dry cells upwind of
it. A gives no difference at all, by construction. If B's difference is small
on this planet's relief, A is the model and the extra pass buys a rain shadow
nobody can see.

Against both, the fraction of land in each biome, against a stated target.

### I-7 — Rivers always run, and half of each one is dry

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18 — rivers may not suit this game
**Step:** dropped, 2026-08-18 — see F-030

#### Why it left the release

The item was scoped as a checkbox, and the checkbox turned out to need an
answer to a question nobody has: whether this game has rivers at all.

Water is written where the coarse map's water surface stands above its ground
surface, which happens in a basin the pit fill raised and below sea level, and
nowhere else. A cell that drains was not raised, so it carries no water.
Measured on a 6,800 m planet at a 32 m coarse cell: **354 of the 785 cells
draining more than a square kilometre carry water**. The other 431 are dry
channels 11 to 37 m deep.

So the switch cannot be built without first deciding what "on" means, and what
"on" means depends on whether rivers are wanted. **The ribbon is the preferred
shape if they stay** — write water wherever the catchment is above a threshold
in square metres, at a depth that follows from the catchment, which
`TerrainColumn.catchment` already carries. Nothing has decided that they stay.

**Rivers keep running exactly as they do now.** No switch is added, and the
routing stays in `buildCoarseMap`, because I-1 measures the longest river on
the largest landmass to tell its two coastlines apart. The flow field is an
instrument in this release even where it is not a feature.

F-015 stays open and is not promoted. **F-030** carries the question the
checkbox turned up.

### I-8 — Erosion is measured in grid units

**From:** F-017, promoted, 2026-08-18
**Findings pulled in:** F-017
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

`erode` lowers a cell by `rate * sqrt(flow) * drop`, where `flow` counts
upstream cells and `drop` is a height difference between neighbours. Neither is
in metres. At a finer level `flow` is four times larger for the same ground and
`drop` is about half, so the errors mostly cancel — and only mostly. Measured
over five large valleys on the 6,800 m planet, floor-to-rim depth came out at
**35.4 m at a 32 m coarse cell against 34.4 m at 16 m** once the relief tier
was stated in metres, and at **11–37 m against 20–43 m** before it was.

`erosionRate` is therefore a knob that means a different amount of cutting on
every planet size and every coarse spacing.

**This is closer to a dependency of I-5 than a finding.** The preview builds at
one level and Apply builds at another. A pipeline whose output moves with the
level makes the preview lie about the thing being tuned.

#### Candidates

**A — Give `erode` the planet.** Divide `drop` by the coarse spacing to get a
gradient in metres per metre, and multiply `flow` by the cell area to get a
catchment in square metres. `erode` has to know the radius, which the coarse
map deliberately does not.

**B — Normalise by level in `buildCoarseMap`.** Scale `rate` by the level
before it reaches `erode`, where the level is already known and the radius is
not needed. `erode` stays a function of a grid.

A is stated in the units the result has and needs the coarse map to learn the
radius. B keeps the boundary where it is and leaves the incision written in
grid units with a correction in front of it.

**The measurement is F-017's**: valley depth on one seed at 32 m and 16 m,
before and after. It should stop moving with resolution.

### I-9 — Twenty-six knobs, and no order among them

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18 — the knobs are overwhelming
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

`PLANET_DEFAULTS` holds 26 entries, the panel draws 24 rows in 7 groups, and
every row is drawn at the same prominence. This release adds more.

Two separate things make it hard rather than merely long. **Some knobs are
derived quantities dressed as inputs** — the panel already answers Radius with
a moved radius, and Coarse cell and Landform across with a level. **And the
interactions are invisible**: Height scale and Land together decide
`maxElevation`, which decides the crust, which is what four of the refusals in
`problems()` are about, and none of that is visible from the rows.

**This item runs last.** Which knobs are worth keeping is a question only the
preview answers, so it waits for I-5.

#### Candidates

**A — Layer them.** A short list at the top that changes what a world is, with
everything else behind a disclosure per group. Nothing is removed, so a knob
somebody relies on is one click away, and the list of what matters is stated
rather than implied by order.

**B — Cut them.** Remove every knob the preview shows to be indistinguishable
across its range, and merge the pairs that always move together. Fewer knobs
and no disclosure to open, at the cost of a range somebody wanted.

**F-019 applies here more than anywhere.** A knob that looks useless on one
planet may be the only interesting one on another, and the close may be
"confirmed as a per-planet knob" rather than a winner.
