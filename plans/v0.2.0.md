# v0.2.0

Minor: the coarse map becomes a stack of maps, and gains an editor to judge it
by. Items may add capability.

Worked through in the three steps
[`HOW-TO-WRITE-PLANS.md`](../HOW-TO-WRITE-PLANS.md) sets out — discuss, try,
implement. Steps 1 and 2 run per item; step 3 runs once, for the whole release,
and starts when every item in the scope has cleared step 2.

**The release is about being able to tune what the planet is made of.** 0.1.2
made one subsystem visible by pausing nine others. This release takes the
subsystem that was paused hardest — the coarse map — and gives it a coastline
worth looking at, the fields a world needs beyond height, and a surface on
which a person can change a number and see the result before a world is built
from it.

**The instrument comes first.** Changing a map knob today reloads the page: a
new device, a level-8 map, and every chunk meshed again. Six of the nine items
below are chosen by looking, and none of them can be judged through that loop.
I-5 is therefore built before the items it is used to settle.

**Every existing seed produces a different world.** I-1 changes where the land
is. Nothing is saved yet, so nothing is lost.

### I-1 — The coastline is a threshold on noise, so the land comes out as blobs

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18 — reference images from a world editor whose
coastlines are ragged and strewn with islands, against ours which are not
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

`continentHeight` sums two tiers of `fbm` and `seaLevelFor` cuts the result at
the percentile that leaves the intended land fraction standing. A percentile
cut through a smooth field draws a smooth curve. The coasts come out rounded,
islands are rare and roughly circular, and there is no archipelago anywhere on
the planet.

The measurements that describe this do not exist yet. What a trial has to
produce, for each candidate and for what ships today:

- **Coastline perimeter over the square root of land area**, which is how
  ragged a coast is, independent of how much land there is.
- **Island count and their size distribution**, an island being a connected run
  of land cells not touching the largest landmass.
- **Longest river on the largest landmass.** Doc 21 already measures the
  continent tier this way — 31 cells on small blobs, 86 on a large landmass —
  so the number is comparable against what is there now.

#### Candidates

**A — Warp the direction before the continent lookup.** Sample a second `fbm`
field and use it to displace the unit direction, then evaluate the continent
tier at the displaced point. A smooth field pushed around by another smooth
field has a folded, filamented level set, so the same percentile cut draws a
ragged curve. Two new parameters: how far the warp pushes, and at what feature
size.

What it keeps: the map stays a pure function of a direction, evaluable at any
point at any resolution, with nothing stored and no build stage added. What it
costs: one more `fbm` evaluation per cell, and the coastline's character is
still a by-product of a threshold rather than something set directly.

**B — Grow the land level by level on the lattice.** Hold a binary land or sea
state per cell and build it up the subdivision hierarchy. At the coarsest level
every cell is land with a hashed probability (**creation**), and a few isolated
cells are seeded away from the rest (**island**). Refining to the next level,
each child inherits its parent's state and then flips with a probability that
depends on how many of its neighbours hold the other state (**growth weight**
and **growth factor**). Repeat to the map's level.

Randomness is hashed from the seed and the cell's lattice coordinates, never
drawn from a running generator, so the result does not depend on the order
cells are visited and a level can be built without building the ones beside it.

What it keeps: the coastline is set directly, at a resolution per level. What
it costs: a stored mask, a build stage, and a field that cannot be evaluated
below the level it was built at.

**Conway's rule itself is not a candidate.** Its birth and survival counts are
written for an eight-neighbour square neighbourhood; on six neighbours, five at
the twelve pentagons, every threshold has to be re-derived. What the reference
images show is the growth-and-refine loop rather than the rule, and B carries
that without inheriting a numbering that does not fit the grid.

#### What decides it

Beyond the three measurements above, one property may settle it on its own.
**A hierarchical process makes a preview an exact prefix of the result.** I-5
previews at a lower level than it applies at. Candidate B built to level 6 is
the first six levels of candidate B built to level 8, so the preview coastline
does not move when Apply is pressed. Candidate A's sea level is a percentile of
the field as sampled, so it shifts with resolution and the preview coastline
moves.

If that holds, the trial has to say by how much: the fraction of cells whose
land or sea state differs between a level-6 preview and the level-8 result, for
each candidate.

### I-2 — Nothing knows how far it is from the sea

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18 — the banded map in the reference images
**Step:** 1

#### The goal

A field holding, for every cell, how far it is to the nearest coastline, signed
so that sea is one way and land the other. Three things read it, which is why
it is one field rather than three calculations:

- **A baseline height.** A shelf that falls away offshore and a plain that
  rises inland, before any noise is added. This is the missing tier: today the
  ground goes straight from a summed noise field to a sea level, so a coast has
  no shelf and an interior no rise.
- **Humidity**, in I-6. Distance to water is what humidity is actually about.
- **Coastal materials**, later — a beach, a shelf, a salt flat are all a band
  at a distance.

The banded map in the reference images is this field drawn with a repeating
palette, which is why its contours follow every inlet.

#### Candidates

**A — Count steps.** A breadth-first walk outward from every cell that has a
neighbour of the opposite state, over the neighbour ring, giving a whole number
of cells. Turned into metres by the coarse spacing. One pass, integer
throughout, and the result does not depend on the order cells come off the
queue, so two machines agree without any care being taken.

**B — Propagate a real distance.** The same sources, but each step adds the
great-circle distance between the two cells rather than one, taken off a
priority queue — `MinHeap` is already here for `fillPits`. The result does not
quantise to the ring, so a band drawn from it is a smooth curve rather than a
staircase.

The trade is visible rather than numerical: A costs one pass and may show its
rings, B costs a heap and does not. **The trial is whether A's quantisation
shows** once the baseline profile is built on it — cell spacing varies 1.41:1
across the sphere, so a step is not the same distance everywhere, and A may
band unevenly at the face centres.

#### The profile

Whichever field wins, the baseline height is a curve over it. Two shapes to
settle inside the trial: a piecewise ramp with named breakpoints in metres
(shelf edge, coastal plain, interior), or one smooth power curve. The ramp is
readable and has more numbers in it; the curve has two numbers and no
breakpoint anyone can point at on the map.

### I-3 — The map is four fields wide and one width deep

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18 — memory is barely used, and the landscape is
poorer for it
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

`CoarseMap` holds `height`, `water`, `flow` and `slope`, all `Float32Array`,
all named as properties, and `toSnapshot` lists them one by one. This release
adds at least six more: the land mask, border distance, baseline height,
temperature, humidity, biome noise and biome.

Two things are wrong with growing it as it stands. **Every field costs four
bytes whether or not it needs them** — a biome is a byte, a mask is a bit, a
step count is two bytes. And **every consumer names every field**, so the
snapshot, the worker protocol and the editor's list of what it can draw all
have to be edited together each time one is added.

The arithmetic, at level 8's 655,362 cells:

| Field | Width | Bytes |
|---|---|---|
| height, water, flow, slope, baseline | `float32` | 2.5 MB each |
| border distance | `int16` | 1.3 MB |
| land mask | `uint8` | 0.65 MB |
| temperature, humidity, biome noise, biome | `uint8` | 0.65 MB each |

Eleven fields at their own widths is about 16 MB. The same eleven at
`float32` is 27 MB. The difference is one more level of map, which is what
actually buys landscape.

#### Candidates

**A — A described set.** A field is a name, a width, a range and a way to color
it, held in a table; the map holds the arrays against those descriptions. The
editor draws a field it is handed without knowing which one it is, and adding a
map is one entry. What it costs: a lookup by name where there is a property
today, and the compiler stops checking that a reader asked for a field that
exists.

**B — Named properties, each at its own width.** The map keeps a property per
field and gains the new ones as properties. Types hold end to end. What it
costs: the editor carries a hand-written list of what it can draw, and adding a
map edits four places.

**The measurement is bytes and copy time**: total resident bytes at levels 8
and 9, and how long `toSnapshot` takes to reach a worker, for each candidate at
the full eleven fields.

### I-4 — The detail term does not know how coarsely it will be drawn

**From:** reading the generator while scoping this release, 2026-08-18
**Findings pulled in:** F-023
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

`TerrainGenerator.columnAt(face, i, j)` takes no level. `lod` exists in
`selectChunks.ts` and stops there. So a chunk drawn two levels coarse evaluates
the same detail field at the same frequency, with its cells four times further
apart, and keeps whatever the field happened to hold at those points.

That is sampling a signal below its own rate. The surface a coarse chunk draws
is not a smoothed version of the fine one — it is a different, arbitrary
selection from it, so the two disagree by the full detail amplitude at a join
and the disagreement changes as a chunk switches level.

Nothing has measured it. What the trial has to produce: the height difference
between one column evaluated at the finest level and at each coarser one, over
a large sample, and how much of the step at a level join it accounts for.

**F-023 belongs here.** Its stated fix is a height bound per chunk rather than
one planet-wide `maxElevation`, and it names the coarse map as the thing that
already knows the height field. A pyramid of tallest and lowest ground per
triangle, at the chunk levels that matter, is tens of kilobytes and answers
both questions.

#### Candidates

**A — Drop octaves.** Pass the level to `columnAt` and stop the `fbm` loop at
the octave whose feature size falls below two cells at that level. The count is
an integer, so a chunk changing level changes octave count in one step and the
surface jumps by that octave's amplitude.

**B — Roll the amplitudes off.** Keep every octave and scale each one against
the cell spacing at that level, so an octave fades out as it approaches the
sampling limit rather than vanishing. The surface moves continuously as a chunk
changes level, and the noise evaluations are not saved.

The trade is stated in the two sentences above: A saves the evaluations and
steps; B is continuous and saves nothing. **The measurement is how far the
surface moves at a level change**, and against it, the evaluations each
candidate leaves in a chunk build.

### I-5 — Tuning a map costs a page reload

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18 — the knobs are hard to form a judgement about,
and there are too many of them
**Findings pulled in:** F-027
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

`ParameterPanel.rebuild()` sets `location.href`. Changing one number reloads
the document, asks for a GPU device, builds a coarse map at the world's own
level, and meshes every chunk in view. Nothing about that loop is wrong for
building a world, and all of it is in the way of choosing a number.

Six items in this release are settled by looking at a map. None of them can be
settled through a loop that costs a reload each time.

**What the panel is today**: 26 knobs, 24 rows, 7 groups, one column beside a
playable world, behind `?panel=1`.

**What it needs to become**: `?panel=1` turns on editor mode, and editor mode
carries panes that show and hide independently — the world knobs, the map
editor, the readouts. A pane is shown because it is being used, not because
the mode is on.

#### The map editor

A pane holding the map stack: a list of fields, one drawn at a time, on a flat
equirectangular canvas beside the existing sphere. Changing a map knob rebuilds
the map and redraws it, and nothing else happens — no device, no mesher, no
chunk. **Apply** builds the world.

Equirectangular is the drawing, never the storage. It is a projection of the
lattice for a person to look at, and it stretches at the poles, which is a
property of the picture rather than of the planet.

#### Candidates

**A — Preview at a lower level, with the same functions.** The map rebuilds at
level 5 or 6 rather than the world's own level, on a short debounce, running
the same code the build runs. Level 5 is 10,242 cells and level 6 is 40,962,
against 655,362 at level 8, so a rebuild is milliseconds where the real one is
seconds.

What it costs: the preview is a lower-resolution map, and whether it is the
same map depends on I-1's answer. A hierarchical land mask previews exactly; a
percentile-cut noise field does not.

**B — Preview at the real level, on a worker, progressively.** Build the map at
the level the world will use, off the main thread, and paint each stage as it
finishes. The preview is the map, and it takes as long as the map takes —
seconds at level 8, and 13.8 s at level 9, which is measured.

What it costs: a knob dragged through ten values queues ten builds, so the
preview needs cancellation, which is the shape of F-001's fix on the residency
loop.

**The measurement is time to redraw after a knob moves**, at the shipped
defaults and at level 9, and against it, how far the preview is from what Apply
produces — the fraction of cells whose value differs, which is the same number
I-1's trial produces.

#### F-027 is what lets any of this be checked here

[`HOW-TO-TAKE-A-FRAME.md`](../HOW-TO-TAKE-A-FRAME.md) carries the four Chromium
flags, the protocol calls and the way to read the pixels back, so the client
runs on a software adapter here and hands a real frame back. What F-027 has
left is the harness itself: about eighty lines in `tools/`, beside the other
checks a session runs before a push.

Every item in this release that is judged by looking goes through it, so it is
built with the editor rather than after it. It settles what is drawn and never
how fast — the frames are a software rasteriser's.

### I-6 — A planet has height and nothing else about it

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18
**Step:** 1

#### The goal

Four more fields, generated, documented, verified and demonstrated, and read by
nothing:

| Field | Holds |
|---|---|
| temperature | how warm a cell is |
| humidity | how wet |
| biome noise | a decorrelating field, so biome edges are not perfectly zonal |
| biome | one index per cell, from the other three and the elevation |

**Nothing consumes them in this release.** They are built so that the model can
be argued about against a picture rather than in the abstract, and so the field
stack has more than one shape of field in it before I-3's decision is locked.

The axis is not a choice: doc 20 fixes the poles at icosahedron vertices 0 and
3 and the prime meridian at vertex 11, so latitude is already defined and every
pentagon sits at a multiple of 36° of longitude.

#### Candidates

**A — Zonal.** Temperature falls from equator to pole and falls again with
elevation at a stated lapse rate, plus noise. Humidity comes from I-2's
distance to water and from temperature — warm air holds more — plus noise.
Biome is a lookup on two axes with elevation and slope overriding at the
extremes.

Three numbers a person can picture: equator temperature, pole temperature,
lapse rate. Deserts land wherever the noise puts them.

**B — Three cells a hemisphere.** The same temperature field, but humidity
follows a banded circulation: wet at the equator, dry at the horse latitudes,
wet at the temperate band, dry at the poles, then carried one step along doc
32's single wind axis before it is read.

Deserts land at the latitudes they land at on a real planet, and the map reads
as a planet rather than as noise. It costs a band model nobody can tune by
looking at one number, and doc 32's wind axis was pinned for clouds rather than
for climate.

**The measurement is where the dry places are**: the fraction of land in each
biome against a stated target, and the latitude distribution of the driest
cells. A is right if the biome fractions come out reasonable; B is right if it
also puts the deserts in bands.

### I-7 — Rivers always run, and half of each one is dry

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18 — rivers may not suit this game, so they need
an off switch
**Findings pulled in:** F-015
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

Two problems, and the switch forces both.

**There is no way to turn rivers off.** `buildCoarseMap` always fills pits,
routes flow and accumulates it. A world that does not want rivers pays for them
and gets them.

**A river is a chain of pools.** Water is written where the coarse map's water
surface stands above its ground surface, which happens in a basin the pit fill
raised and below sea level, and nowhere else. A cell that drains was not
raised, so it carries no water. Measured on a 6,800 m planet at a 32 m coarse
cell: **354 of the 785 cells draining more than a square kilometre carry
water**. The other 431 are dry channels 11 to 37 m deep.

So "keep rivers, with a checkbox" cannot be answered without saying what a
river is.

#### Candidates

**A — The switch alone, and rivers are pools.** Off skips the fill, the route
and the accumulation, and water is sea level and nothing else. On is what runs
today: a chain of ponds in an incised valley. A dry wash between pools is a
real landform, and this planet has no rainfall to say otherwise.

**B — The switch, and a river is a ribbon.** Off is the same. On also writes
water wherever the catchment is above a threshold in square metres, at a depth
that follows from the catchment. `TerrainColumn.catchment` already carries the
number, so it is one comparison per column.

**The measurement is F-015's own**: the fraction of cells draining more than a
square kilometre that carry water, and against it, the extra columns B writes
water into.

### I-8 — Erosion is measured in grid units

**From:** F-017, promoted, 2026-08-18
**Findings pulled in:** F-017
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

`erode` lowers a cell by `rate * sqrt(flow) * drop`, where `flow` counts
upstream cells and `drop` is a height difference between neighbours. Neither is
in metres. At a finer level `flow` is four times larger for the same ground and
`drop` is about half, so the errors mostly cancel — and only mostly. Measured
over five large valleys on the 6,800 m planet, floor-to-rim depth came out at
**35.4 m at a 32 m coarse cell against 34.4 m at 16 m** once the relief tier
was stated in metres, and at **11–37 m against 20–43 m** before it was.

`erosionRate` is therefore a knob that means a different amount of cutting on
every planet size and every coarse spacing.

**This is closer to a dependency of I-5 than a finding.** The preview builds at
one level and Apply builds at another. A pipeline whose output moves with the
level makes the preview lie about the thing being tuned.

#### Candidates

**A — Give `erode` the planet.** Divide `drop` by the coarse spacing to get a
gradient in metres per metre, and multiply `flow` by the cell area to get a
catchment in square metres. `erode` has to know the radius, which the coarse
map deliberately does not.

**B — Normalise by level in `buildCoarseMap`.** Scale `rate` by the level
before it reaches `erode`, where the level is already known and the radius is
not needed. `erode` stays a function of a grid.

A is stated in the units the result has and needs the coarse map to learn the
radius. B keeps the boundary where it is and leaves the incision written in
grid units with a correction in front of it.

**The measurement is F-017's**: valley depth on one seed at 32 m and 16 m,
before and after. It should stop moving with resolution.

### I-9 — Twenty-six knobs, and no order among them

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18 — the knobs are overwhelming
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

`PLANET_DEFAULTS` holds 26 entries, the panel draws 24 rows in 7 groups, and
every row is drawn at the same prominence. This release adds more.

Two separate things make it hard rather than merely long. **Some knobs are
derived quantities dressed as inputs** — the panel already answers Radius with
a moved radius, and Coarse cell and Landform across with a level. **And the
interactions are invisible**: Height scale and Land together decide
`maxElevation`, which decides the crust, which is what four of the refusals in
`problems()` are about, and none of that is visible from the rows.

**This item runs last.** Which knobs are worth keeping is a question only the
preview answers, so it waits for I-5.

#### Candidates

**A — Layer them.** A short list at the top that changes what a world is, with
everything else behind a disclosure per group. Nothing is removed, so a knob
somebody relies on is one click away, and the list of what matters is stated
rather than implied by order.

**B — Cut them.** Remove every knob the preview shows to be indistinguishable
across its range, and merge the pairs that always move together. Fewer knobs
and no disclosure to open, at the cost of a range somebody wanted.

**F-019 applies here more than anywhere.** A knob that looks useless on one
planet may be the only interesting one on another, and the close may be
"confirmed as a per-planet knob" rather than a winner.
