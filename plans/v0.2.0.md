# v0.2.0

Minor: the map editor. One capability, and the knobs that reach it.

Worked through in the three steps
[`HOW-TO-WRITE-PLANS.md`](../HOW-TO-WRITE-PLANS.md) sets out — discuss, try,
implement. Steps 1 and 2 run per item; step 3 runs once, for the whole release,
and starts when every item in the scope has cleared step 2.

**The release is about turning a knob and seeing the answer.** Changing any
setting reloads the document: a new device, the whole coarse map, and every
chunk in view generated, meshed and uploaded. Twenty-six knobs sit behind that
loop, and nobody can form a judgement about a number through it.

**The maps themselves do not change here.** No new field, no new coastline, no
climate. This release builds the instrument; [`v0.3.0`](v0.3.0.md) uses it, and
carries the map work that was split out of this file.

**The editor draws the maps at full resolution.** A map at the shipped level is
`3.4 s` of work against a whole world rebuilt, so nothing is previewed at a
reduced level and what is on screen is the map. **Apply** is the separate button
that takes the maps to the terrain.

## Items

### I-1 — Tuning a map costs a page reload

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18 — the knobs are hard to form a judgement about,
and there are too many of them
**Findings pulled in:** F-027
**Step:** 2

#### The problem

`ParameterPanel.rebuild()` sets `location.href`. Changing one number reloads the
document, asks for a GPU device, builds a coarse map at the world's own level,
and meshes every chunk in view. Nothing about that is wrong for building a
world, and all of it is in the way of choosing a number.

**What the panel is today**: 26 knobs, 24 rows, 7 groups, one column beside a
playable world, behind `?panel=1`.

**What it becomes**: `?panel=1` turns on **editor mode**, and editor mode
carries panes that show and hide independently — the world knobs, the map
editor, the readouts. A pane is shown because it is being used, not because the
mode is on.

#### The map editor

A pane holding the map stack: a list of fields, one drawn at a time, on a flat
equirectangular canvas beside the existing sphere. Changing a map knob rebuilds
the map and redraws it, and nothing else happens — no device, no mesher, no
chunk. **Apply** builds the world.

Equirectangular is the drawing, never the storage. It is a projection of the
lattice for a person to look at, and it stretches at the poles, which is a
property of the picture rather than of the planet.

#### What one map costs

Measured at the shipped defaults, with the grid kept between runs:

| stage | level 7 | level 8 | level 9 |
|---|---|---|---|
| grid — no knob changes it | 171 ms | 413 ms | 1,642 ms |
| height field | 353 ms | **1,361 ms** | 5,268 ms |
| sea level | 18 ms | 79 ms | 348 ms |
| erosion | 355 ms | **1,585 ms** | 8,031 ms |
| flow | 78 ms | 391 ms | 1,841 ms |
| slope | 11 ms | 34 ms | 137 ms |
| **knob to map** | **815 ms** | **3,450 ms** | **15,625 ms** |

A timing, so it moves run to run; read the shares rather than the values. Two
things decide the shape of this item. **The grid is not rebuilt** — nothing but
the coarse spacing changes it, so it is built once and kept. And **erosion and
the height field are 85% of what is left**, which is where both candidates aim.

#### Candidates

**A — Re-run only what the knob reaches.** The stages form a chain, and most
knobs do not enter it at the top. Land only reaches sea level and below, so it
skips the height field. Erosion rate reaches erosion and below, so it skips the
height field and sea level. Hold each stage's output and recompute from the
first one the knob touches.

What it gives: the knobs that skip the height field come back in about `2.0 s`
instead of `3.4 s`. What it does not give: erosion is downstream of almost
everything, so almost every knob still pays for it.

**B — Show it before it is finished.** Run the whole chain on a worker and paint
each stage as it lands — the raw height field first, then the coastline once sea
level is known, then the eroded surface, then the rivers. The first useful
picture is on screen after the height field rather than after all of it.

What it gives: every knob feels the same, and the one a person is turning is
usually visible in the first stage. What it costs: cancellation, because a knob
dragged through ten values queues ten runs, which is the shape of F-001's fix on
the residency loop.

**The measurement is two times, not one**: how long until there is a picture
worth looking at, and how long until it is the final map — for a knob at the top
of the chain and a knob near the bottom.

#### F-027 is what lets any of this be checked here

[`HOW-TO-TAKE-A-FRAME.md`](../HOW-TO-TAKE-A-FRAME.md) carries the four Chromium
flags, the protocol calls and the way to read the pixels back, so the client
runs on a software adapter here and hands a real frame back. What F-027 has left
is the harness itself: about eighty lines in `tools/`, beside the other checks a
session runs before a push.

It settles what is drawn and never how fast — the frames are a software
rasteriser's.

### I-2 — A field cannot be drawn without knowing what it means

**From:** writing I-1, 2026-08-18 — the editor has to paint a field it is handed
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

`CoarseMap` holds `height`, `water`, `flow` and `slope` as four `Float32Array`
properties and says nothing else about them. To paint one, the editor needs
three things a typed array does not carry: **what range it spans**, **how to
turn a value into a color**, and **what to call it on screen**.

The four want different treatments. Height reads as a terrain ramp against sea
level. Water is one color and a mask. Flow spans several orders of magnitude and
needs a log scale, or the whole planet is black with a few white threads. Slope
is a grey ramp.

**This is the drawing half only.** Choosing a storage width per field — a biome
as one byte rather than four — belongs with the fields that need it, and is
[`v0.3.0`](v0.3.0.md)'s I-3.

#### Candidates

**A — A description beside the map.** A table of field name, label, range and
color ramp, held next to `CoarseMap` and read by the editor. The map class does
not change. What it costs: two places to edit when a field is added, and nothing
makes them agree.

**B — A described field on the map.** The map holds its fields as a set of
descriptors, each carrying its array and how to read it, and the editor
enumerates them. Adding a field is one entry. What it costs: a lookup where
there is a property today, and the compiler stops checking that a caller asked
for a field that exists.

**The measurement is what it takes to add the fifth field.** v0.3.0 adds at
least four more, so the trial is to add one under each candidate and count the
places that have to change — against the type safety B gives up.

### I-3 — Twenty-six knobs, and no order among them

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18 — the knobs are overwhelming
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

`PLANET_DEFAULTS` holds 26 entries, the panel draws 24 rows in 7 groups, and
every row is drawn at the same prominence.

Two separate things make it hard rather than merely long. **Some knobs are
derived quantities dressed as inputs** — the panel already answers Radius with a
moved radius, and Coarse cell and Landform across with a level. **And the
interactions are invisible**: Height scale and Land together decide
`maxElevation`, which decides the crust, which is what four of the refusals in
`problems()` are about, and none of that is visible from the rows.

**This item is judged in the engine and runs last.** Which knobs are worth
keeping is a question only the editor answers, so both candidates go in behind
the editor and the losing one comes out in this same release.

#### Candidates

**A — Layer them.** A short list at the top that changes what a world is, with
everything else behind a disclosure per group. Nothing is removed, so a knob
somebody relies on is one click away, and the list of what matters is stated
rather than implied by order.

**B — Cut them.** Remove every knob the editor shows to be indistinguishable
across its range, and merge the pairs that always move together. Fewer knobs and
no disclosure to open, at the cost of a range somebody wanted.

**F-019 applies here more than anywhere.** A knob that looks useless on one
planet may be the only interesting one on another, and the close may be
"confirmed as a per-planet knob" rather than a winner.

### I-4 — Every chunk is built as though the tallest mountain stood in it

**From:** F-023, promoted, 2026-08-18
**Findings pulled in:** F-023
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

`selectChunks` reaches `horizonAngle(eye) + horizonAngle(peak)`, and `peak` is
one planet-wide `maxElevation` — how tall the tallest ground anywhere is. So
every chunk that could hold visible ground **if the planet's highest mountain
were standing in it** is selected, and almost none of them are.

Measured on the un-paused world, `maxElevation` about 120 m and its own horizon
about 1.3 km: the eye-height scene goes from **286 chunks to 552**, and from
**2.4 s to 3.9 s** to fill a view. Roughly half the chunks built at eye height
sit in a ring that is mostly below the horizon, built and uploaded for nothing.

It is the correct conservative bound — nothing visible is ever dropped, which is
what v0.1.2's I-4 restored — and it is paid on every world with relief, every
time the player moves.

**It belongs in this release because it is the other half of the loop.** The
editor makes a map arrive in seconds; Apply still rebuilds the world, and this
is the largest single piece of that.

#### Candidates

**A — A height bound per triangle, from the coarse map.** The map already holds
the height field, so the tallest ground under each triangle can be taken once at
world creation, for the chunk levels the walk visits. The walk already goes
parents before children, so each triangle uses its own peak instead of the
planet's. Tens of kilobytes.

**B — A height bound per triangle, from the chunks already built.** Keep the
planet-wide bound for a triangle nothing is known about, and replace it with the
true peak of any chunk that has been generated. Costs no precomputation and no
storage beyond a number per chunk, and it is only right for ground somebody has
already visited.

A is exact from the first frame and needs the selection to read the coarse map,
which it does not today. B needs nothing new and is wrong exactly where a player
is going rather than where they have been.

**The measurement is the bench**: chunks selected and seconds to fill a view, at
eye height and at 60 m, against the 286 and 552 above — and against it, that no
chunk holding visible ground is ever dropped.
