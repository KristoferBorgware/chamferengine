# v0.1.1

Patch: fixes to what 0.1.0 shipped. No item adds a capability.

Worked through in the three steps
[`HOW-TO-WRITE-PLANS.md`](../HOW-TO-WRITE-PLANS.md) sets out — discuss, try,
implement. Steps 1 and 2 run per item; step 3 runs once, for the whole release,
and starts when every item in the scope has cleared step 2.

**The release is about scale.** Everything in it came from standing on the
ground and finding that nothing reads at the size it should: the sky is used up
within two metres of altitude, the clouds are a dome you can touch, and the
ground has hills where it should have landforms.

**Old share codes stop working.** I-3 changes the subdivision depth, and a share
code carries the address at a depth. Nothing is saved yet, so nothing is lost,
but every link made from 0.1.0 names a different place after this release.

### I-1 — The atmosphere is used up by two metres of altitude

**From:** visual inspection, 2026-08-18 — "the atmosphere is very close and
extremely thin, so thin that you can only see the sky scattering at a certain
precise altitude"
**Findings pulled in:** F-011
**Step:** 1, complete

#### The problem

`SKY_SHADER.ts` multiplies the camera's altitude by `6371000 / 1700 = 3747.6`
before placing it in the atmosphere it draws.

| Height here | Lands at on Earth | |
|---|---|---|
| 0 m | 0 km | |
| 1.6 m, eye height | 6.0 km | already a mountain-top sky |
| 16 m | 60 km | above the atmosphere |
| 150 m, the tallest ground | 562 km | far above it |

**Half the air is gone by 1.48 m of altitude**, and the whole 60 km atmosphere
is used up by 16 m. The ground reaches 150 m, so terrain stands above the sky
that lights it. The window where the sky reads correctly is roughly 0 to 2 m.

[Doc 32](../docs/32-sky-clouds-and-moon.md) decided to run the scattering on a
fictional Earth-sized atmosphere and take **only the sun direction** from the
world. It never asked for the camera's height to be scaled. That multiply
arrived with Project 12 and contradicts the document.

#### What both candidates have to carry

**The atmosphere is a property of the planet, not a constant in a shader.** It
is currently written down twice and parameterised neither time:

- `packages/engine/src/sky/ATMOSPHERE.ts` holds Earth's numbers in TypeScript
  and is read by nothing but its own test.
- `SKY_SHADER.ts` holds `PLANET`, `TOP`, `H_RAY`, `H_MIE`, `B_RAY`, `B_MIE` and
  `G_MIE` as WGSL `const` declarations, which is what actually draws and which
  cannot be changed while the program runs.

The next world may be Mars-like. Whatever is chosen below, the numbers reach the
shader through the uniform it already has — `settings : vec4f` uses two of its
four slots today.

#### Candidates

**B1 — Earth's atmosphere, with a per-planet mapping.** The world declares a sky
scale; the shader lifts the camera's real altitude by it into the modelled air.
A Mars-like world gets its own coefficients, its own scale height and its own
scale factor.

**B2 — The planet declares its own atmosphere, in world metres.** An atmosphere
top, a scale height, and scattering coefficients raised so the zenith optical
depth lands where it is wanted. No mapping and no lift: the camera's real height
goes straight in, and the air ends at the altitude the world says it ends at.

#### What separates them

The **horizon-to-zenith path ratio**, `sqrt(2*pi*R/H)`, which is what reddens a
sunset and keeps the zenith darker than the horizon.

| Atmosphere | Scale height | Ratio |
|---|---|---|
| Earth | 8 km | 70.7 |
| Scaled to this planet | 2.1 m | 70.8 |
| 200 m tall | 26.7 m | 20.0 |
| 400 m tall | 53.3 m | 14.2 |
| 800 m tall | 106.7 m | 10.0 |

The first two rows are the finding. **An atmosphere scaled in proportion with
the planet reproduces Earth's sky geometry exactly**, because the ratio depends
on `R/H` and `H` scales with `R`. Doc 32 is right that this needs air 3,748
times denser, and the density is invented in either candidate; what the document
does not say is that the scaled version is geometrically identical. Proportional
scaling does not fail on the physics. It fails because a **16 m** atmosphere is
one a player walks out of.

So B2's trade is stated plainly: **choosing how tall the air is chooses how
strong sunsets are.** B1 keeps Earth's sunsets on every world forever, which for
a thin-aired planet is wrong in the other direction.

#### Where step 1 left it

The pair is agreed. **B2 is preferred**, and the demo still decides, because B2
carries a free number that nothing here has settled: how tall the air is. That
number is the sunset, and it cannot be chosen from the table above — 400 m and
800 m are both defensible and they look different.

#### Trial

**Built:** `demos/atmosphere-scale.html`. Both candidates are correct
arithmetic, so the question is which sky is worth having, and only a picture
answers it.

The two halves are handed the same scattering, the same sun and the same camera
ray. They differ in the air they are given and in nothing else, and both carry
**the same optical depth straight up** — B2's coefficients rise by exactly what
its scale height falls by — so the colour overhead starts equal and the geometry
is what separates them.

**What it already shows, before anyone looks at it by eye:**

| Camera at | B1 thinks it is at | B2 is at |
|---|---|---|
| 1.6 m | 6.00 km | 1.6 m of 400 m |
| 46 m | 171 km | 46 m of 400 m |
| 770 m | 2,885 km | 770 m of 400 m, above the air |

At 46 m of real altitude — a modest hill on the worked planet — **B1 is black
with a thin blue rim** and B2 is a daytime sky. That is the reported defect,
drawn twice on one screen.

**Still to settle by eye:** how tall B2's air should be. The horizon-to-zenith
path ratio is 70.7 on Earth, 20.0 at a 200 m atmosphere and 10.0 at 800 m, and
that ratio is the sunset.

**The demo** is standalone HTML in `demos/`, with no dependencies,
and it has four things to move:

1. **B1 against B2**, side by side, on the same sun and the same camera.
2. **B2's atmosphere height**, from 100 m to 1,700 m, which is the sunset.
3. **The sun's elevation**, from overhead to below the horizon, because a
   candidate that reads well at noon and grey at dusk has not been tested.
4. **The camera's altitude**, from the ground to orbit. The whole defect is
   that altitude broke the sky, so a demo that only looks from sea level would
   have passed the broken version.

It reports the zenith optical depth and the horizon-to-zenith path ratio beside
the picture, so what is being looked at has a number attached.

**F-011 does not bind here.** The demo draws with WebGL 2 rather than WebGPU,
which the development container does render, so the pictures above were checked
before the page was handed over. The engine's own sky still cannot be seen here;
this page can.

### I-2 — The clouds are a dome, and refilling them stalls the frame

**From:** visual inspection, 2026-08-18 — "the clouds are just too close, they
should be further up, and they feel like textures on a dome"
**Findings pulled in:** F-014, F-011
**Step:** 1, complete

#### The problem

`buildCloudMesh` emits **one flat hexagon per point at one radius**. No
thickness, no sides, no bottom. It is a texture on a dome, and it is drawn as
one.

The deck sits at 220 m, which is 1.47 times the tallest ground, and three
seconds of flying away. The furthest cloud visible from eye height is 896 m;
the same measurement on Earth is 143 km.

**And the field already stalls the frame.** Refilling it costs **12.7 ms on the
thread that draws, every 0.7 seconds** — three quarters of a frame's budget,
twice a second. That is F-014, and it exists today with flat clouds.

| Level | Puff | Points on the sky | Carrying cloud | Refill, one layer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5, in use | 64 m | 10,242 | 2,813 (27%) | 12.7 ms |
| 6 | 32 m | 40,962 | 11,263 (27%) | 52.7 ms |
| 7 | 16 m | 163,842 | 45,051 (27%) | 192.6 ms |

#### What both candidates have to carry

- **Two decks**, raised, with smaller puffs.
- **The field and its mesh are built on a worker.** Everything asked for is
  unaffordable on the main thread — level 6 over four layers is 211 ms — and
  the field is already a pure function of the wind angle and the seed, so it
  moves the way chunks moved. This closes F-014.

Raising the deck buys more sky than distance:

| Deck height | Seen to | Share of sky in reach | Puffs in view, level 6 | level 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 220 m, today | 896 m | 5.7% | 2,347 | 9,387 |
| 600 m | 1,330 m | 13.0% | 5,343 | 21,371 |
| 900 m | 1,533 m | 17.3% | 7,090 | 28,357 |
| 1,400 m | 1,757 m | 22.6% | 9,249 | 36,997 |

Going from 220 m to 900 m triples the sky within reach and lifts the viewing
distance only 1.7 times. That is the right gain here: the complaint is about
seeing the dome's edge, not about seeing far.

#### Candidates

**A — Hex prisms.** One point per column, with a base radius and a top radius
from the noise, so a puff has height and a silhouette. **6.4 times today's
triangles**, and one noise evaluation per point as now. Flat-topped within a
puff, so a plateau rather than a billow.

**B — Cloud cells over shells.** Sample the noise in three dimensions over four
to eight shells and emit the exposed faces, culling the interior ones the way
the terrain mesher culls them. **12.7 times the triangles over four shells, 22.2
over eight.** A cloud then has a shape from below, from the side, and from
inside. Four to eight times the build cost of a prism.

Triangles decide nothing: 570,000 for the whole sky at level 6 over four shells
is one terrain view, and frustum culling already cuts it. **The build cost is
the whole question**, which is why the worker move comes first.

#### What B needs said out loud

Doc 32 holds that a cloud has **no address** — no cell ID, no chunk, no layer —
and that withholding it is what keeps clouds cosmetic by construction. B builds
clouds out of things shaped like cells at shells.

It is compatible, and the reason is exact: what makes a thing storable is the
**cell ID**, because the delta store, the side table, interest routing and edit
messages are all keyed by it. A mesher taking a face, an offset and a shell, and
returning triangles, never mints one. If B wins, doc 32 gains a sentence saying
so, or its next reader will believe the rule was broken.

#### Where step 1 left it

**Chosen: B.** Clouds are built from cells over shells, the way the ground is.
A is recorded as the candidate not taken: a slab has height and an edge, and it
still has a flat lid and a flat floor, which is a sticker seen from a different
angle.

#### What step 2 still has to settle

Choosing B settles the shape and none of the numbers:

- **Two deck heights.** 600 m and 1,400 m are the ends of the measured table
  and both are defensible.
- **Which level the puffs come from.** Level 6 is a 32 m puff, level 7 a 16 m
  one, and the second costs four times the first.
- **How many shells deep a deck runs.** Four against eight, which is 12.7 times
  today's triangles against 22.2.

Every one of those is judged by looking up, so they are knobs on the trial
branch rather than numbers argued here.

#### What step 3 owes doc 32

[Doc 32](../docs/32-sky-clouds-and-moon.md) holds that a cloud has no cell ID,
no chunk and no layer, and that withholding the address is what keeps clouds
cosmetic by construction. B builds them out of things shaped like cells at
shells, so the document gains a sentence saying why the rule still holds: what
makes a thing storable is the **cell ID**, because the delta store, the side
table, interest routing and edit messages are all keyed by it, and a mesher
taking a face, an offset and a shell never mints one.

Without that sentence the next reader finds cells in the sky and concludes the
rule was broken.

#### Trial

Not started. On the trial branch from I-4, with the three numbers above as
knobs. **F-011 applies** — judged on real hardware.

### I-3 — The ground has no landforms at the size a person reads

**From:** visual inspection, 2026-08-18 — "the landscape has no variation, no
mountains, no valleys"
**Findings pulled in:** F-010
**Step:** 1, complete

#### The problem

The terrain is **not proportionally flat**. 131 m of relief on a 10.7 km
circumference is 1 : 82, against Earth's 1 : 2004 — so this planet is already 25
times more rugged than Earth for its size. Adding amplitude fights that reading
rather than fixing it.

What is wrong is the size of a landform against the size of a person, and how
far a person can see:

| Tier | Longest feature | Shortest | Height it carries |
|---|---|---|---|
| Continent | 2,125 m | 266 m | 29.3 m sd |
| Relief | 283 m | 18 m | 10.2 m sd |
| Detail | 28 m | 4 m | 1.2 m sd |

The tallest ground measured is **54 m — thirty people**. An Earth mountain is
five hundred to sixteen hundred. And the horizon at eye height is **74 m**, so
a landform wider than about 150 m can never be seen whole from the ground: a
200 m peak at 74 m is a wall, not a view.

#### The decision taken

**The planet grows to a 6,800 m radius at subdivision depth 13**, which holds
the block at 1.000 m and doubles the horizon to 147 m. The alternative — keeping
the planet and spending the amplitude — is recorded as the candidate not taken.

| Radius | Horizon | Walk round | Depth for 1 m blocks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.7 km | 74 m | 2.1 h | 11 |
| 6.8 km | 147 m | 8.5 h | 13 |
| 25 km | 283 m | 31 h | 15 |
| 111 km | 596 m | 138 h | 18, over the 17-level ceiling |

Every doubling of the horizon costs four times the radius and two levels of
depth.

#### Growing the planet on its own makes it worse

| World | Horizon | Relief in it | Worst | Gradient |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R 1,700 m, D11, heightScale 120 | 74 m | 17.7 m | 46.5 m | 0.239 |
| R 6,800 m, D13, heightScale 120 | 147 m | **10.8 m** | 33.2 m | **0.073** |
| R 6,800 m, D13, heightScale 480 | 147 m | 41.5 m | 116.7 m | 0.281 |
| R 6,800 m, D13, heightScale 720 | 147 m | 62.3 m | 172.4 m | 0.422 |

Noise is sampled from unit direction vectors, so a frequency is features per
sphere rather than per metre. Quadrupling the radius makes every landform four
times longer in metres while `heightScale` still says 120 m, and the horizon
only doubles, because it goes as the square root of the radius. The result is
**half the slope in view, on a planet that takes four times as long to cross**.

So the amplitude has to rise with the radius, and how far is the question step 2
answers.

#### Candidates

**A — heightScale 480.** Restores today's steepness at twice the viewing
distance. Relief in view 41.5 m, worst 116.7 m, gradient 0.281.

**B — heightScale 720.** Visibly mountainous: relief in view 62.3 m, worst
172.4 m, gradient 0.422 — a slope of 23 degrees on average across the horizon.

**Rejected, and recorded so nobody tries it again:** the radius alone at
heightScale 120. It halves the visible slope.

#### What depth 13 drags along

- **Address 33 bits**, and 55 of 64 with the planet and layer fields. Comfortable
  against the 64-bit word, and **not comfortable against the `number` an ID is
  stored as** — a `number` counts exactly to 53 bits, which depth 12 already
  reaches. Nothing breaks at planet 0, which is the only value any shipped world
  uses, but the margin implied by "55 of 64" does not exist. F-018.
- **The crust cap flips.** The taper would allow 1,740 layers at D13, but the
  10-bit layer field allows 1,024, so the addressing binds where the taper bound
  at D11. 1,024 m of crust is ample for terrain reaching a few hundred metres.
- **Chunk level moves 6 to 8.** At D13 with chunk level 6 a chunk is `m = 128`,
  8,385 slots and 128 m across — fifteen times today. At chunk level 8 it is
  back to `m = 32`, 561 slots and 32 m, which is what every number Project 16
  measured assumes.
- **F-010 gets worse.** The level-of-detail chain bottoms out at the twenty
  icosahedron faces however far away the camera is. On a planet four times
  larger the floor is four times coarser in angle and the wasted generation
  grows with it. Pulled in to be decided alongside the radius rather than after.

#### Built

**The planet is 6,801 m at depth 13**, and the default height scale is **480**.
720 is one slider away on the panel, which is where the choice between them is
made.

`maxElevation` stopped being a number anyone sets. Elevation is linear in the
height scale, so over 3,000 places on the worked seed:

| Height scale | Deepest | Tallest | Spread | Relief in a 148 m horizon |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 120 | −73 m | 54 m | 127 m | 10.7 m mean, 33 worst |
| **480** | −295 m | **216 m** | 511 m | **41.7 m mean, 116 worst** |
| **720** | −442 m | **324 m** | 766 m | **62.3 m mean, 171 worst** |

The tallest ground is **0.45** of the height scale and the spread from sea floor
to peak is **1.06** of it, at every amplitude tried. So both follow from the
height scale and neither is asked for.

#### Back to step 1 — the amplitude was the wrong knob

**480 and 720 are indistinguishable standing on the ground.** Two screenshots at
eye level, one of each, and neither of us could say which was which. That sends
the item back to step 1, because the choice step 2 was set up to make turns out
not to be the choice.

The reason is the shape of what is in view, and it splits in two. Fit a plane
across the whole visible ground and measure both halves separately: how far the
ground **tilts** across the view, and how far it departs from that tilt, which
is the only part that reads as a landform.

| Setting | Tilt across the view | Landform on top of it |
|---|---|---|
| heightScale 480 | 32.8 m | 15.1 m |
| heightScale 720 | 49.4 m | 22.4 m |

Both numbers rise by the same half, and **the ratio does not move**: 0.46 and
0.45. Amplitude multiplies the whole height field, so it makes a slope steeper
without making a hill. From the ground, a uniformly steeper world looks like the
same world.

What decides the ratio is **wavelength against the horizon**, and it is the
number the growth to 6,801 m moved the wrong way. Noise is sampled from a unit
direction, so a frequency is features per sphere: quadruple the radius and every
landform is four times longer in metres, while the horizon only doubles, because
it goes as the square root of the radius.

| Planet | Horizon | One relief feature | Features across the view | Tilt | Landform |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R 1,700 m, D11 | 76 m | 283 m | 0.27 | 54.7 m | 31.0 m |
| R 3,400 m, D12 | 107 m | 567 m | 0.19 | 41.9 m | 21.3 m |
| R 6,801 m, D13 | 152 m | 1,134 m | 0.13 | 32.8 m | **15.1 m** |

Same height scale, same seed. **The bigger planet has half the landform**, and
that is what the amplitude was raised to cover. It covered the slope and not the
shape.

Turn the wavelength instead and the ratio moves, at a fixed height scale of 480:

| Change | Tilt | Landform | Landform ÷ tilt |
|---|---|---|---|
| shipped, `reliefFrequency` 6 | 32.8 m | 15.1 m | 0.46 |
| `reliefFrequency` 12 | 52.4 m | 29.8 m | 0.57 |
| `reliefFrequency` 24 | 76.1 m | 57.9 m | 0.76 |
| `reliefFrequency` 48 | 82.9 m | 94.3 m | **1.14** |
| detail 40 m, frequency 60 | 34.9 m | 30.3 m | 0.87 |
| detail 80 m, frequency 60 | 44.4 m | 54.5 m | 1.23 |

At `reliefFrequency` 48 one relief feature is 142 m, which is one horizon: a
hill you walk over rather than a hillside you stand on.

#### Candidates

**A — the coarse map's relief tier is stated in metres.** `reliefFrequency`
becomes a feature size, the way I-5 made the coarse spacing a size and doc 21
now states its resolution. A feature of about 150 m at the shipped amplitude
gives 82.9 m of tilt and 94.3 m of landform. Every planet then keeps the same
landform size whatever its radius, which is the property the growth to 6,801 m
broke. The cost is that the coarse map at a 32 m cell is sampling a 142 m
feature with four cells across it, so the shape of a hill is described by very
few numbers — and rivers run on this tier, so their catchments change with it.

**B — the detail term carries the landforms.** Raise `detailAmplitude` from 5 m
to somewhere near 40 m and leave the coarse map alone. It is pure noise
evaluated per column, so it needs no rebuild, no coarse map change and nothing
of the rivers moves. The cost is that detail is added **after** the water
decision, by design: the coarse map says where water is and the detail moves the
ground under it. At 5 m that is invisible. At 40 m a hill would rise through a
lake and a hollow would sit dry below sea level.

Neither is free of the other's problem, and the pair wants a measurement of what
B does to the water before it can be chosen.

#### Built — A, and the frequency is gone from the world description

**Every noise frequency is now asked for as a size in metres and divided by the
radius on the way in.** `reliefFeature` and `detailFeature` are the knobs;
`reliefFrequency` and `detailFrequency` are what the engine is handed. The rule
is doc 08's, measured on pure noise in `verification/noise.js` section 8, and it
is the same rule I-5 applied to the coarse map's resolution.

**The octave count follows from the size.** Each octave is half the width of the
one above, so the relief tier runs however many halvings fit between the largest
landform and a fixed floor of **64 m**. The floor is a number of metres and not
a multiple of the coarse cell, because tying it to the cell would give a 16 m
map one octave more than a 32 m map — the same seed growing different hills at
different resolutions, which is exactly what I-5 removed. A world whose coarse
cell is too wide to carry its smallest hill is refused.

The property that buys: **landforms are 280 m down to 70 m on every planet**,
from 1,700 m to 27,203 m of radius. The frequency moves from 6.07 to 97.15 to
hold them there.

#### The defaults, and why the amplitude came down

| | Landform across | Height scale | Tilt | Landform | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Before | 1,136 m | 480 | 32.4 m | 14.8 m | 0.46 |
| **Shipped** | **280 m** | **200** | **32.4 m** | **27.5 m** | **0.85** |
| Same width, old amplitude | 280 m | 480 | 78.1 m | 65.3 m | 0.84 |

The shipped pair puts **the same slope underfoot and 1.9 times the landform on
it**. Shortening a wavelength at a fixed amplitude makes everything steeper, so
480 at 280 m is 78 m of fall across a 152 m view — a 27 degree hillside
everywhere. Dropping the height scale to 200 holds the steepness where it was
and spends the whole gain on shape. Both are sliders.

Elevation is still linear in the height scale, and the landform size moves the
two constants by about a tenth: the tallest ground is **0.50** of the height
scale where it was 0.45, and the spread from sea floor to peak is **1.15** where
it was 1.06. `maxElevation` and `groundSpan` carry the margin.

**`tools/bench.ts` no longer restates the world.** It builds a `PlanetSettings`
and reads the shape and both option objects off it, so the divergence that let
the bench measure 480 while the client built 120 cannot happen again. A knob can
still be moved for one run by name: `BENCH_heightScale=720`.

That is not tidiness. **A column is written from the crust top downward**, so
every metre of empty sky above the tallest peak is a layer evaluated on every
column of every chunk. Setting the tallest ground by hand and setting it too
high is a generation cost nobody would connect to the knob that caused it.

**What the bigger planet costs**, from `npm run bench` at the shipped settings:

| Scene | Chunks | Cells | Triangles a frame | Build | Worst chunk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Eye height, flat ground | 277 | 141,777 | 351,882 | 2,993 ms | 31.9 ms |
| Eye height, relief | 383 | 196,336 | 385,082 | 2,145 ms | 9.1 ms |
| Standing at the shore | 238 | 122,115 | 412,386 | 3,305 ms | 40.3 ms |
| Sixty metres up | 450 | 230,560 | 250,838 | 2,629 ms | 39.3 ms |
| Orbit | 28 | 14,338 | 81,589 | 143 ms | 8.5 ms |

**About 11 ms a chunk**, against 12.4 ms on the small planet — a chunk is still
32 cells across and still costs what it did. What grew is how many are held:
277 against 196 at eye height, because the horizon doubled. On seven workers a
view fills in about 0.44 s.

World creation is **3.2 s** at a 32 m coarse cell, which is I-5's cheaper
candidate and the default until that item chooses.

#### Trial

**The pair is agreed: 480 against 720**, and both are in the engine now.

A demo standing on the ground at both amplitudes, at eye height, looking level —
the height a person judges a slope from. It runs at the new radius and depth, so
the horizon in it is the 147 m one rather than today's 74 m, and the two
amplitudes are a key apart so the same view can be compared without moving.

`bench` runs at the new radius and depth as part of the same trial, to check the
chunk numbers Project 16 recorded still hold once a chunk covers different
ground.

### I-4 — A planet is not described in one place

**From:** step 1 of I-1, on the observation that the next world may be Mars-like
**Step:** 1, complete

#### The problem

There is no single object that says what a world is. The numbers are spread
across a client file, a shader, and three engine modules: `RADIUS`, `DEPTH`,
`MAX_ELEVATION`, `COARSE_LEVEL`, `CLOUD_HEIGHT`, `CLOUD_LEVEL`, `SKIRT_CELLS`,
`DETAIL`, the terrain options, and seven atmosphere constants inside WGSL.

Every other item in this release changes one of those numbers, and each of them
lives somewhere different.

#### Why it is here

Not a fix on its own. It is what makes the other items land somewhere rather
than adding more constants to more files, and it is what a second world needs
before it can exist at all. It goes first in step 3, because I-1, I-2 and I-3
all write into it.

#### What is already settled, and is a requirement rather than a candidate

Two rules the project has taken decide the shape, so neither is up for a trial.

**The description is composed of per-subsystem blocks, not one flat type.**
[`CODE-STYLE.md`](../CODE-STYLE.md) states the property: *a server imports
`chamfer/addressing` and pulls in nothing that mentions a GPU*. One type naming
an atmosphere means everything importing it names an atmosphere. `WorldShape`
and `TerrainOptions` are already blocks of this kind.

**It is authored in metres, and the machine numbers are derived.** A person sets
a block size and a radius; the subdivision depth follows, and the radius moves
to make the block size exact. That is [doc 06](../docs/06-world-sizing.md)'s
rule and this project's invariant 7 — *block size is fixed at world creation,
radius absorbs level rounding* — so a description holding a depth rather than a
block size has the dependency backwards. The same applies to the coarse map's
level from I-5, the crust's layer count, and the chunk level.

#### The open question: where does a description come from

There are no save files, so a world is made from what a person types on the
landing page, which today is a seed and nothing else.

**A — Named presets.** A short list of complete worlds, chosen on the landing
page. The URL carries a preset name and a seed. A new world means a new entry
in the list.

**B — A parameter form, with the world in the URL.** The landing page exposes
the numbers a person sets — radius, block size, atmosphere height, cloud height,
terrain amplitude, coarse spacing — and the URL carries them, so a world is a
link. Presets become starting points on the same form.

#### What separates them

A is small, keeps the landing page a seed box, and is enough to give this
release somewhere to put its numbers.

B costs more and pays for itself inside this release: **every other item here is
choosing a number by eye** — how tall the air is, how high the decks sit, 480
against 720, 32 m against 16 m. With B those are dialled in the real client on
real terrain rather than in four separate demos.

Against B: a world becomes a URL with a dozen numbers in it, and every one of
them can be set to something that does not work — a crust that does not reach
the sea floor, a coarse spacing finer than the blocks. B needs the description
to refuse a world it cannot build, and that is most of its cost.

#### Where step 1 left it

**Chosen: B.** The landing page carries the knobs and the world travels in the
URL. A is recorded as the candidate not taken: it would have been enough to
give this release somewhere to put its numbers, and it would have left every
other item choosing its number in a demo that is not the engine.

B carries one duty A did not: **a description has to refuse a world it cannot
build.** A crust that does not reach the sea floor, a coarse spacing finer than
the block, an atmosphere below the tallest ground — each is a URL a person can
type, and each has to come back as a message rather than a broken planet.

#### Trial

Not started. Settled by a check rather than a picture. Write the description,
put I-1's atmosphere, I-2's cloud settings, I-3's radius and amplitude and
I-5's spacing into it, and confirm two things: that no item needs a special
case, and that walking the import graph from `chamfer/addressing` still reaches
nothing about a sky. Both are answerable without touching the engine.

#### What choosing B does to the other items

The knobs are what the rest of the release is judged on, and step 3 has not
happened yet, so the trials need somewhere to stand.

- **I-1** does not need a world. Its demo is standalone HTML that draws sky and
  nothing else, and it stays in `demos/` afterwards.
- **I-2, I-3 and I-5** cannot be judged without terrain, a coarse map and the
  lattice. Rebuilding those in standalone HTML would be trialling an
  approximation of the engine rather than the engine.

So those three are trialled **in the engine, on `master`, behind `?panel=1`**.
Both sides of each candidate go in as a knob, the choice is made by looking, and
the losing side comes out before the release closes.

### I-5 — The coarse map's resolution is tied to the planet's size

**From:** step 1 of I-3, working out what depth 13 costs
**Step:** 1, complete

#### The problem

The coarse map is built at a subdivision level, and a level means a different
spacing on a different planet. I-3 makes the planet four times larger, so the
map that carries continents, rivers and lakes drops to a quarter of its
resolution against the ground without anybody asking for that.

Measured, on the same seed, at three levels:

| Level | Cells | Spacing at R 6,800 | One field | Build | Land | Stranded | Longest river |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | 163,842 | 64 m | 0.6 MB | 0.9 s | 30% | 0 | 87 cells, 5.6 km |
| 8 | 655,362 | 32 m | 2.5 MB | 3.4 s | 30% | 0 | 178 cells, 5.7 km |
| 9 | 2,621,442 | 16 m | 10.0 MB | 13.8 s | 30% | 0 | 363 cells, 5.8 km |

**The map's conclusions do not move.** Land is 30% at every level, nothing is
stranded at any of them, and **the longest river is 5.6 to 5.8 km whichever
level draws it**. Resolution does not decide where the water goes. It decides
how finely the channel is described: the same river is 87 cells at level 7 and
363 at level 9.

So the question is not whether rivers work. It is **how wide a river is**, in
blocks, because a coarse cell is the finest a channel can be:

| Spacing | A river channel is |
|---|---|
| 64 m | 64 blocks wide — wider than a road, closer to a lake |
| 32 m | 32 blocks wide |
| 16 m | 16 blocks wide — still a major river |

There is no level here that produces a stream. Small water does not exist on
this map at any resolution, and would have to come from the fine terrain, which
carves nothing today.

#### What both candidates have to carry

**The resolution is stated in metres and the level follows from the radius.** A
level is a property of the grid, not of the world, and tying a world's rivers to
one means every change of planet size silently changes them. This is the same
shape as I-1's atmosphere and belongs in I-4's description.

#### Candidates

**A — 32 m coarse cells.** Level 8 on the new planet: 655,362 cells, 2.5 MB a
field, **3.4 s** of world creation.

**B — 16 m coarse cells.** Level 9: 2,621,442 cells, 10 MB a field, **13.8 s**
of world creation, which is a loading screen — and
[doc 29](../docs/29-what-runs-where.md) has the client regenerating this map on
every load rather than downloading it.

#### Noticed while measuring

The river threshold counts **cells** upstream, not area, so the same physical
catchment scores four times higher at each finer level. Rivers as a share of
land read 9.0%, 11.6% and 13.2% across the three, and part of that is the
threshold rather than the terrain. Whatever level is chosen, the threshold wants
stating in square metres.

#### Trial

**Built, and waiting on a look.** The pair is 32 m against 16 m, and the panel's
*Coarse cell* slider is both of them: the spacing is asked for in metres and the
level follows from the radius, so the same slider gives level 8 on a 6,800 m
planet and level 6 on a 1,700 m one.

Two things had to be fixed before the comparison meant anything.

**The panel's ground knobs reached nothing.** *Height scale*, *Detail*, *Land*
and *Crust reaches* were read into `PlanetSettings` and never passed on, so the
client built every world at the engine's defaults — a 120 m height scale, not
I-3's 480 — while `tools/bench.ts` measured 480 and reported different numbers
for the same settings. The worker had `terrain: {}` in its setup message, which
is the copy that actually builds what is drawn. `PlanetSettings` now hands over
a `WorldShape`, a `CoarseMapOptions` and a `TerrainOptions`, and both the main
thread and the worker take them.

The crust went the same way, and further. The client asked for
`maxCrustDepth(13)`, which is **1,740 layers** — the depth at which a column
pinches shut, not the depth anybody wanted. The panel says 900 m, the layer
field holds 1,024, and the bench measured 900. Every chunk was therefore 561 by
1,740 slots, **1.95 MB against 1.0 MB**, with the extra 840 layers written by
the bulk rock fill and thrown away. `PlanetSettings.shape()` now applies both
caps.

**The catchment was a cell count.** `TerrainColumn.flow` carried the map's
number, which is how many cells drain through a cell — four times higher at each
finer level for the same ground. Comparing a channel at 32 m against the same
channel at 16 m was therefore comparing two different quantities. It is now
`TerrainColumn.catchment`, in square metres, and `rivers.js` section 7 measures
what holds across resolutions and what does not.

#### What the two spacings measure

`verification/rivers.js` section 7, and the engine at the shipped settings —
R 6,801 m, depth 13, 280 m landforms, height scale 200, seed `chamfer`:

| | 32 m (level 8) | 16 m (level 9) |
|---|---|---|
| Cells | 655,362 | 2,621,442 |
| One field | 2.5 MB | 10 MB |
| World creation | **3.1 s** | **13.9 s** |
| Land | 29.8% | 29.9% |
| Sea level | 0.2226 | 0.2226 |
| Largest catchment | 2.52 km² | 2.52 km² |
| Valley floor to rim | mean 35.4 m | mean 34.4 m |
| Valley width 5 m above the floor | 32–158, mean 78 blocks | 19–155, mean 63 blocks |
| Land carrying standing water | 9.63% | 9.88% |
| Big channels carrying water | 50% | 54% |

The planet-scale answers are the same at both, down to an identical sea level
and an identical largest catchment. **The valleys are now the same depth as
well** — 35.4 m against 34.4 m, where on the long-wavelength terrain 16 m cut a
quarter deeper. Stating the relief tier in metres is what closed that gap: the
tier stops at 70 m, which both maps carry, so neither has fine content the other
lacks. What is left is width: **78 blocks against 63 on average**, and the
narrowest channel is 32 blocks against 19.

The cost is a **loading screen**: 13.9 s against 3.1 s, and
[doc 29](../docs/29-what-runs-where.md) has every client regenerating this map on
every load rather than downloading it.

#### What is left to decide

Stand at a river bank at each spacing and say which valley reads better:

- `?panel=1&radius=6800&coarseSpacing=32`
- `?panel=1&radius=6800&coarseSpacing=16`

Press <kbd>E</kbd> to drop to eye level. The losing spacing stays available as a
slider; only the default changes.

### I-6 — There is no way to change a number and look at the result

**From:** step 2 of I-1, 2026-08-18 — every decision left in this release is a
number chosen by eye, and each one currently means an edit and a rebuild
**Step:** 1 — accepted, knob list gathered as the other trials run

#### The problem

Deciding a coarse map's resolution, a planet's radius, a subdivision depth or a
cloud's height by describing them to each other is slow and inexact. The
question every one of them asks is *what does this look like*, and the only way
to answer it is to change the number and stand in the world.

#### What is being built

**A collapsible panel down the right side of the planet page**, holding the
numbers that define a world and the ones that dress it. A change that needs the
planet rebuilt rebuilds it — this is a bench, not a feature, and a two second
wait costs nothing against reading the answer.

It is scaffolding for deciding, not part of what the engine is for. It goes on
the removal list the same way the first-light scaffolding did, and it comes out
when it stops earning its place.

#### The knobs

Gathered as each trial turns one up. A knob earns a place by having changed an
answer in this release, or by being a number nobody can pick from a table.

**The world, and a change rebuilds it**

| Knob | Range | Why it is here |
|---|---|---|
| Seed | text | Every other knob reads differently on a different world |
| Planet radius | 1,700 m to 25 km | I-3. Decides the horizon, and the horizon decides what a landform can be |
| Block size | 0.5 m to 2 m | Sets the subdivision depth with the radius; doc 06 has the radius absorb the rounding |
| Terrain height scale | 60 m to 900 m | I-3's 480 against 720 |
| Detail amplitude | 0 m to 40 m | What the near ground does between coarse cells |
| Coarse cell spacing | 8 m to 64 m | I-5's 32 m against 16 m, in metres rather than as a level |
| Land fraction | 0.1 to 0.6 | How much of the surface is above the sea |
| Crust depth | in metres | Has to reach below the deepest sea before anyone digs |
| Chunk size | 16, 32, 64 cells | Chunk level, in cells rather than as a level |

**The sky and the air, and a change is immediate**

| Knob | Range | Why it is here |
|---|---|---|
| Atmosphere height | 50 m to 2 km | I-1. This is the sunset |
| Zenith optical depth | 0.05 to 0.6 | How thick the air reads at all |
| Mie share | 0 to 0.4 | Haze against blue |
| Sun elevation and day length | — | Every sky judgement depends on where the sun is |

**The clouds, and a change refills them**

| Knob | Range | Why it is here |
|---|---|---|
| Deck heights, two of them | 200 m to 2 km | I-2 |
| Puff size | 8 m to 64 m | I-2's level, in metres |
| Shells per deck | 1 to 8 | I-2's depth; 1 is today's flat hexagon |
| Cover and wind rate | — | How much sky is filled, and how fast it moves |

**What is being drawn, and a change is immediate**

| Knob | Range | Why it is here |
|---|---|---|
| Detail distance | 1 to 5 chunk widths | How far full resolution reaches |
| Skirt depth | 0 to 4 cells | Whether a seam shows |
| Draw distance | — | What the budget line is measuring |
| Frustum culling on or off | — | Shows what culling is worth, live |

#### Candidates

None. There is no second way to let someone turn a number and look at the
result, and the panel is not a choice between two designs — it is the
instrument the other choices are made with.

#### Where it sits

**On `master`, behind `?panel=1`.** The normal page is unchanged and the planet
draws as it does today; the parameter adds the panel and nothing else.

It is built **before** the items it serves, which puts one item's step 3 ahead
of the queue. That is deliberate and recorded here rather than done quietly:
I-2, I-3 and I-5 are judged on it, so it cannot come after them. Nothing else
from those items is implemented until each has chosen.

#### Built

`packages/client/src/PlanetSettings.ts` and `ParameterPanel.ts`, reached with
`?panel=1`.

**A world is what the query string says it is.** Every knob has a default, and a
default is the number 0.1.0 shipped, so the page with no parameters draws the
same planet it drew before. Only what differs from a default is written back, so
a link stays short and says what was chosen.

**Every knob is in metres or in cells.** Subdivision depth, chunk level and
coarse level are not knobs — they follow from a size and a radius, and the
radius moves to whatever makes the block size exact, which is doc 06's rule.
Checked against the two worlds this release cares about:

| Asked for | Depth | Radius | Chunk | Chunk level | Coarse | Crust |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,700 m, 1 m blocks | 11 | 1,700 m | 32 m | 6 | 16 m, level 7 | 435 layers |
| 6,800 m, 1 m blocks | 13 | 6,801 m | 32 m | **8** | 16 m, level 9 | 700 layers |

The first row is 0.1.0 exactly. The second is every number I-3 worked out by
hand, including the chunk level moving from 6 to 8, arrived at from a radius and
a block size alone.

**It refuses a world it cannot build.** A 32 m crust under 600 m of ground
answers *the crust reaches 32 m and the ground spans about 630 m, so the sea
floor would fall through the bottom of the world*, and the rebuild button goes
dead. Five such checks: the depth ceiling, the crust against the ground, a
coarse cell no coarser than a block, detail taller than the ground it moves, and
a chunk that is the whole face.

**It comes up before the device does.** A browser that will not give WebGPU
still shows the panel, so the answer to a planet that will not draw is to change
it and try again rather than to edit a file.

#### Trial

Its own trial is the release: **every number this release chooses is chosen on
it.** A knob that decided something stays; a knob nobody touched comes out
before the panel ships.
