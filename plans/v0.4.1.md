Patch: the three findings v0.4.0 turned up, and a way to look at what the
selection is deciding.

Every item here comes from something observed while building the edit path.
Nothing adds a capability to the world; the one new control draws what the
engine is already doing so a judgement about it can be made by looking.

## I-1 — The selection reads the ground the seed made

**From:** [F-067](../FINDINGS.md)
**Step:** 1

### The problem

`ChunkPeaks` holds the tallest and the lowest ground under every triangle of
the chunk hierarchy, built once from the coarse map. The map is a picture of the
**generated** world: no placed block is in it and none can be.

**The finding understated what that costs.** It is not only a level-of-detail
question. `selectChunks` reads the peak three times, and two of them decide
whether a chunk is drawn at all:

- **Over the horizon.** A chunk is dropped when its own ground cannot poke back
  round the curve. A tower's chunk is judged by the hillside under it.
- **Out of view.** The chunk gets a bounding ball stretched from its own lowest
  ground to its own highest, and a ball that misses the frustum prunes the
  triangle **and all four children under it** in one test. A build standing out
  of the top of that ball is outside the volume that decides its fate.
- **How far away.** The distance is measured to the chunk's ground, which then
  picks the level of detail.

So a tall build can be **culled outright**, not merely coarsened: the ball is
sized to the seed's hillside, the tower stands out of the top of it, and when
the hillside leaves the screen the tower goes with it.

### Candidates

**A — The store raises the pyramid.** A chunk holding changes reports its
highest placed block and its lowest, and `ChunkPeaks` takes the larger of that
and the map's figure, up through every ancestor. The store already knows which
chunks those are; a layer is a radius, so the metres are arithmetic. Costs one
number per changed chunk, maintained on write, and a pass over the store when a
world is opened.

**B — A chunk holding changes is never culled.** Keep the pyramid as it is and
exempt edited chunks from the horizon and frustum tests. Simpler, and it gives
up the pruning that makes the walk cheap: a triangle that cannot be refused
takes its four children with it, so a scattering of edits across a planet
un-prunes a large part of the hierarchy.

## I-2 — Every click rewrites the whole world's record

**From:** [F-068](../FINDINGS.md)
**Step:** 1

### The problem

The browser's database holds one record per world, carrying every chunk's
changes together, so writing one block packs and stores all of them. It grows
with the size of the world rather than with the size of the change.

### Candidates

**A — One record per chunk.** Keyed by the world's name and the chunk's key
together, with the header in a record of its own. A click writes one chunk;
opening a world is a range query over its name. This is the shape
[`ARCHITECTURE.md`](../ARCHITECTURE.md) already commits to for the hosted store
— chunk ID to a blob, one `get` and one `put` — so it is that migration arriving
early rather than a new idea.

**B — Write on a timer rather than on every click.** Keep one record per world
and save at most a few times a minute, plus once when the page is hidden. Fewer
writes for the same shape, and it trades a durable click for a cheaper one: a
browser closed at the wrong moment loses the last few seconds of building.

## I-3 — A change is written into two or three chunks and nothing says so

**From:** [F-069](../FINDINGS.md)
**Step:** 1

### The problem

A chunk draws only the cells it owns — the border rule settles that, and the
mesher enforces it. But to decide whether to put a face on the edge of a cell it
does own, a chunk has to know whether the neighbour is solid, and that neighbour
may belong to the chunk next door.

**For generated terrain this is not a problem, and the reason is exact.** The sampler does not fetch the neighbour's data. It re-evaluates the
column from the seed, and gets the same blocks the neighbour's own array holds,
because terrain is a pure function of the address. Every chunk computes the same
answer independently, so they cannot disagree and nothing has to be shared.

**An edit is the first thing in the world that is not a function of the
address.** Re-evaluating gives the ground the seed made, not the wall a player
built, so the assumption underneath the border rule stops holding. A change is
therefore written into every chunk whose triangle contains the cell, and 17% of
a chunk's slots sit on a border.

What is left is that the store is a few per cent larger than the number of cells
anybody changed, and that `count` reports records rather than cells — neither
wrong, both easy to read as a bug.

### Candidates

**A — Report both.** A `cells` beside `count`, and the duplication stated where
it is written. Keeps the read path as it is: a chunk's records arrive with the
job and need no gathering.

**B — Store canonically and gather at the job.** One record per cell, in the
chunk that owns it, and a job carries its chunk's rows plus those of the chunks
sharing its rim. Smaller store, one place to write, and it moves work to the
read — every job now assembles three rows instead of one, and each record has to
carry which chunk it was written under.

## I-4 — Nothing draws the volumes the culling tests against

**From:** the owner, judging the selection by eye
**Step:** 1

### The problem

Two different balls decide whether a chunk reaches the screen, and neither is
visible from the camera that decides them.

The **selection's** ball is built in `selectChunks` from the triangle's own
tallest and lowest ground, widened by a margin that grows with distance. It
decides whether a chunk is asked for at all.

The **patch's** ball comes back with the mesh — the ball the built geometry
actually fits inside — and decides whether a resident chunk is drawn this frame.

The selection reads pessimistic and there is no way to see where the slack is:
in the margin, in the peak the triangle is credited with, or in the ball's own
shape. A frame of the balls answers that directly, the same way the frozen view
marker answers what the selection could see.

### Candidates

**A — Two switches, one per volume.** Each draws its balls as three rings on the
axes, at the chunk's own level, over the world and inside its depth. Two rather
than one because the question is usually which of the two is refusing a chunk,
and a picture with both in it does not answer that.

**B — One switch and a colour per volume.** Fewer rows on the panel and both
volumes always drawn together. Reads as clutter at any distance, since a
selection is hundreds of chunks and each ball is a metre of ring against a
kilometre of ground.

## I-5 — The margin beyond the view is metres on every chunk

**From:** I-4's picture, and the owner reading it
**Step:** 3 — **built**

### The problem

The culling keeps a margin of ground beyond the edge of the screen, so turning
finds it already built. **That margin was never applied to the view.** The cull
tested the camera's own 65 degrees, and `selectChunks` added the margin's
tangent times the distance to **each chunk's own ball**.

So "keep 25 degrees beyond the screen" was spelled as "make every chunk's test
volume much larger than the chunk". Drawing the volumes is what showed it: the
rings were so large they read as straight lines across the whole view.

> **[measured]** `tools/trial-bounds.ts`, on the shipped world from where a
> player stands. The ball each chunk was culled by ran **8.2×** the half-width
> of the chunk inside it, at every level of detail, and **76.4%** of the average
> radius was the margin — 190 m of it, against 30 m for the triangle's own width
> and 29 m for its floor-to-peak span.

Three things follow, and only the first is obvious. A ball is a sphere, so 8.2×
the radius is **512× the volume**, and every cubic metre of it votes to be kept.
The margin grows with distance, so a chunk a kilometre out wears a 466 m coat.
And it is added in **every** direction, including at the near plane, the far
plane and behind the camera, where no margin was ever wanted.

### What it is now

The cull frustum is built from a **widened projection** and the ball is the cap
of ground the triangle holds and nothing else.

The vertical angle is widened enough that the horizontal one grows by the margin
too. The aspect ratio turns a vertical widening into a smaller horizontal one,
and the sides are where turning brings ground on, so taking the vertical figure
alone would keep less to the sides than the knob asks for.

> **[measured]** Same script and same viewpoint. The ball against its own
> chunk's half-width falls from **8.2×** to **1.13×** at lod 3 and about 1.2×
> typically, and the selection from **946** chunks to **876**. The margin still
> does its job: **631** chunks are on screen and it keeps **245** more.

What is left of the radius is the ground: **50.9%** the triangle's own width and
**49.1%** half its floor-to-peak span.

**The finest chunks are still credited with more span than they have**, because
the pyramid holding those figures stops two levels above the map. That is
[F-070](../FINDINGS.md), left deliberately: the default world is cut one level
below the cap so it barely bites, and closing it costs 7 MB against the 437 KB
the table takes now.

## I-6 — A chunk is a wedge, and a ball cannot hold one

**From:** the owner, reading I-4's picture and asking what happens when a
player digs
**Step:** 3 — **built**

### The problem

Everything a chunk is tested against was a **ball**: the volume `selectChunks`
tests before asking for a chunk, the volume `ChunkRenderer` tests before drawing
a resident one, and the volume `CascadeShadow` tests before drawing one into a
shadow map. A ball is a fine fit for the ground, which is a thin cap a few tens
of metres across and a few tens of metres tall.

**It is the wrong shape for the world the chunk actually holds.** A chunk is a
small triangle on the surface extruded straight down through the crust, and how
far down depends on how deep anybody has dug. **A ball cannot be grown downward
alone.** Grow it to reach the bottom of a shaft and it reaches the same distance
sideways, upward and behind, and every cubic metre of that votes to be kept.

This is a voxel engine, so the shaft is not a corner case. It is the second
thing a player does.

> **[measured]** `tools/trial-bounds.ts`, section 4, on the shipped world.
> A 76 m chunk over a 1,232 m crust. Holding just the ground it needs a box
> 44 m deep, and a ball round that is **3×** the volume. Dug a quarter of the
> way down it is **13×**. Dug to the bottom the ball is **640 m** in radius
> against a 76 m chunk — **148×** the volume of the box, all of it ground the
> cull can no longer refuse.

There was a second half to it, in the store rather than the geometry.
`DeltaStore` recorded how far a change reached so the selection could widen the
volume past what the coarse map knows, and it **left breaking out** on the
reasoning that taking ground away never puts geometry outside the volume the
ground already had. That is wrong: the walls of a hole are geometry standing
where the map says solid ground is. A player at the bottom of their own mine was
in a chunk whose test volume stopped at the surface far above them.

### What it is now

`Box` — a centre, three perpendicular unit axes and a half-width along each.
`Frustum.holdsBox` tests one against six planes the way `holds` tests a sphere,
with the box's reach along each plane's normal standing in for the radius.
`chunkWedge` builds the one a chunk wants: long axis along the chunk's own
direction from the planet's centre, from the inner surface's nearest corner out
to the outer surface, and both widths the radius of the disc the triangle sits
in.

Three volumes changed, not one.

- **The selection.** `ChunkCull` is a box test now, and `selectChunks` builds
  the wedge from the peak and trough the pyramid holds.
- **The renderer.** `ArrayMeshSink` measures itself along axes it is handed
  rather than the world's, so a `ChunkMesh` carries the box its own triangles
  fall inside — three dot products a vertex against three comparisons.
- **The shadow.** `CascadeShadow.boxOf` takes a box: how far it reaches along
  the light and how far it stands off the axis are each a sum over its three
  axes, a cosine for the first and a sine for the second. A shaft under a low
  sun barely widens the second.

And `DeltaStore` now stretches a chunk's reach for **every** change, breaking as
well as placing.

The diagnostic follows: `BoundsRenderer` draws each volume as its twelve edges
rather than three rings.

> **[measured]** Same script and viewpoint, undug ground. A ball round each
> box would be **3.8×** its volume at the finest level and 2.8× at the
> coarsest. That is what the shape is worth before anybody picks up a shovel.

### What it does not change

The margin is still an angle on the view (I-5), the peak pyramid still stops two
levels above the map ([F-070](../FINDINGS.md)), and the horizon test is still a
dot product against a direction. None of those is a shape question.

## I-7 — An edit at a chunk border reaches the chunk that stores it and not the one that draws it

**From:** the owner, digging into a mountain and getting triangle-shaped ridges
along every chunk edge and holes through to the far side of the planet
**Step:** 3 — **built**

### The problem

**A chunk meshes more cells than its triangle contains.** Its rim cells ask the
ring around them whether to draw a side face, and the apron draws that ring
outright — and a cell one step past the rim sits inside the *neighbouring*
chunk's triangle. `ChunkColumnSampler` generates those on demand from the
terrain function, which is a pure function of the seed and has never heard of
anything a player did.

`chunksHolding` hands a change to every chunk whose triangle **contains** the
cell. That is the right set for the store and the wrong one for the mesher.

> **[measured]** `tools/probe-seam-edit.ts`, depth 8 cut at chunk level 4. A
> chunk holds **153** slots and reads **54** more from one step past its rim.
> Of those 54, a change was handed to it for **0**.

Two symptoms, one cause, and they are the two the owner saw. **A wall left
standing**: break a block just across a boundary and the neighbour's apron goes
on drawing the seed's cap there, so mining across a chunk edge leaves a one-cell
ridge along it — a triangle outline of ground nobody removed. **A hole to see
through**: a rim cell asking whether to draw a wall is told there is rock where
the tunnel is, emits nothing, and the far side of the planet shows through.

### The second bug, underneath

`chunksHolding` found its candidates by asking the cell's own ring which chunks
owned them. That misses a chunk at its own triangle's **corner**: the only
neighbours a corner has inside its triangle sit on that triangle's two edges, so
both are shared, and where the border rule awards both to lower-keyed chunks the
triangle whose corner it is never appears at all.

> **[measured]** Same script, over every chunk of one face: **155 of 39,168**
> cell-and-chunk pairs went unreported. **0** now.

### What it is now

`chunksReading` is the wider set — every chunk holding the cell **or any
neighbour of it** — and the store routes and invalidates by it.

`chunksHolding` **descends the triangles** rather than asking the ring.
Triangles nest, so a chunk containing a point has an ancestor containing it at
every level above, and at most six paths stay live however deep the cut.

`applyDeltas` returns what fell outside the triangle instead of dropping it, and
`ChunkColumnSampler` writes those records over the columns it generates,
recomputing the band the same way a chunk's own columns get it. It also matches
each record through `cellRepresentations` now, so a row owned by a chunk on the
other side of a face edge cannot path-match this triangle by coincidence.

And a chunk's cull reach is stretched for **every** reader, not just the owner:
the apron draws the ring past the rim, so a shaft dug just across the boundary
is geometry this chunk puts on the screen.

> **[measured]** `packages/engine/tests/edit/seamEdits.test.ts`. The invariant
> the whole scheme rests on, stated directly: for every cell past the rim, the
> column this chunk **generates** equals the column the chunk that **owns** it
> holds, blocks and band alike, after breaking and placing both. Three of the
> six tests fail if the sampler is not given the records.

## I-8 — Looking around is a button held down, and the cursor sits over the world

**From:** the owner
**Step:** 3 — **built**

### The problem

Turning the view meant holding the left button and dragging. Looking around is
what a player does constantly and clicking is what they do occasionally, so the
constant one cost a button.

Worse, it made **every press ambiguous**. The same gesture starts a look and
breaks a block, and the only thing telling them apart is how far it travelled —
five pixels, a rule the player has to know. Under it, a small drag breaks a
block nobody meant to break.

And the arrow cursor sat over the world, pointing at a cell the crosshair was
not aiming at.

### What it is now

Clicking the canvas captures the mouse. Moving it then turns the view with no
button down, and the browser takes the cursor off the canvas — which is the
other half of the same thing. A press is a press: left breaks, right places, no
slop test.

`Escape` gives the cursor back, which is how the parameter panel is reached, and
the readout's last line says so while the mouse is captured.

**The press that captures also acts.** The usual rule elsewhere is that the
first click only captures, so that clicking into a window to focus it does not
fire a weapon. It was built that way and it is wrong here: the mouse is let go
far more often than it looks — `Escape`, a slider on the panel, any move to
another window — so that rule spends a real click every time the player comes
back, and the symptom is *nothing happened, so I clicked again*. Breaking one
block too many is undoable; a control that ignores you is not.

The four cases are one table, `clickIntent`, rather than a chain of tests inside
an event handler: the whole of the input's behaviour is in them, so they are
written where they can be read and tested.

### What still drags

**Touch never captures**, so a finger keeps the drag path, the pinch and the
touch controls exactly as they were. So does a browser that refuses to capture
— and refusal arrives two ways that both have to be caught: the older shape
fires `pointerlockerror`, the newer one rejects a promise. Left uncaught that
is an unhandled rejection *and* a fallback that never arms, which is a canvas
where clicking does nothing at all: no look, and no block broken either.
Refusal is usually temporary — a browser will not re-capture within about a
second of letting go — so it lapses after three seconds and the next click tries
again.

**Not verified in a frame.** Headless Chromium refuses to capture the pointer,
so what a run here checks is the fallback: no unhandled rejection, and the drag
and the click still working.

## I-9 — The player's collision reads the seed, so they stand on ground they mined out

**From:** the owner, standing in the air after removing the blocks under them
**Step:** 3 — **built**

### The problem

`player.step` was handed the **terrain generator** as its probe. Terrain is a
pure function of the address; a player's changes are not. So the player's body
was moving through the world as it was before anybody touched it.

Three symptoms, one wire. Mine out the ground under yourself and you keep
standing on it. Place a block and walk through it. Dig a tunnel and be stopped
by rock that is no longer there.

The client already had one answer to *what is here* — the same `blockAt` the
aiming walk, the outline and a click all go through — and the player was the
one thing not asking it.

### What it is now

`worldBlocks` holds both shapes of the question, a cell and a point in space,
and everything asks it: the aiming walk, the outline, a click, collision,
floating, and whether the camera is under water.

The store is taken as a **function** rather than a value, because it is replaced
when a saved world finishes loading — a probe holding the empty one would answer
from the seed forever, which is the same bug arriving a second way.

The floor of the world stays under everything a record can say: the deepest
layer reads as bedrock however it was written, so no edit digs through the
bottom.

## I-10 — The arm is six blocks and nothing can change it

**From:** the owner
**Step:** 3 — **built**

### What it is now

**Reach** is a knob, 2 to 64 blocks, under **The player**.

**In blocks, not metres**, so it means the same thing on a world of 1 m blocks
and one of 4 m blocks: how many blocks along the line of sight the arm gets to.

## I-11 — The first click does nothing and the second one works

**From:** the owner, on I-8
**Step:** 3 — **built**

### The problem

I-8 built the conventional rule: the press that captures the mouse only
captures. That is right in a game you enter once and play for an hour, and wrong
here.

**The mouse is let go far more often than it looks.** `Escape` drops it, a
slider on the parameter panel drops it, and so does any move to another window
and back. Each of those costs the next real click. From the player's side that
is a control that ignores them at random — *nothing happened, so I clicked
again* — and it read the same for breaking and for placing, because both go
through the same press.

### What it is now

A press acts whether or not it is also the press asking for the mouse. Breaking
one block too many is undoable; a control that ignores you is not.

The four cases are one table, `clickIntent`, tested on its own: a captured mouse
acts, an uncaptured one asks **and** acts, a mouse the browser refused falls back
to the drag, and a finger is always the drag. Nothing else in the handler
decides anything.

### What else can look like this, and does not change

Two other things make a click do nothing, and both say so in the readout rather
than being silent. **Placing needs room above the cell aimed at** — that is
v0.4.0's rule, aim at the cell you want to build on and the block goes on top —
and the outline is not drawn when there is no room. And a **frozen view** aims
at nothing by design.

## I-12 — A change disappears once its chunk is drawn coarse

**From:** the owner — *delta changes disappear at a certain distance, this was
not ok*
**Step:** 3 — **built**

### The problem

**A chunk key is `face × 4^chunkLevel + path`**, so the same triangle is a
different number at every level. The delta store is filed at one level — the
finest — and the mesh pool asked it for a chunk's records using that chunk's
*own* key. For the chunk being drawn at full detail those agree. For every
coarser one the key means some other triangle entirely, and what came back was
nothing.

> **[measured]** A store cut at chunk level 4, one edit written. `rowsFor` gives
> the owning chunk **1** row and the chunks covering the same ground one, two
> and three levels out **0**, **0** and **0**.

So a change was drawn while its chunk was the finest and vanished the moment the
selection dropped that chunk a level — which is a *distance*, not an event, and
reads as edits evaporating as you walk away and coming back as you return.

Two more faults sat behind it, and neither could show while the first one
starved the path.

**A slot was read against the wrong level.** A slot is a rank inside a triangle
whose side the chunk level sets, and the store files every record against the
finest. A coarse chunk decoding one against its own level reads a different cell
— so even handed the rows it would have written the change somewhere else.

**Nothing dropped the coarse chunk.** An edit invalidated `selectionId` at the
finest level only, so the chunk actually on screen — whichever level the
selection picked — was never rebuilt.

**And the test that should have caught all three was modelling a chunk that does
not exist.** It built its coarse chunk with the *finest* chunk level and a
reduced depth. A real one drops both, and the two always sum to the finest —
which is what keeps every chunk the same number of slots while four of them
become one.

### What it is now

`rowsUnder(key, chunkLevel)` answers for a chunk at any level. A coarse triangle
contains exactly the fine ones whose path begins with its own, so it is a filter
over what the store holds rather than a walk over the `4 ^ lod` fine chunks
under it — there may be a million of those and there are only ever as many rows
as chunks somebody has touched. The reading map is filtered the same way, so a
coarse chunk inherits every row its fine chunks read past their own rims.

`applyDeltas` decodes a slot against `chunk.chunkLevel + lod`, which is the
level the record was filed at however coarse the chunk reading it is.

`coarseChunkKey` converts a key between levels, and an edit drops the chunk
covering that ground at **every** level rather than only the finest.

> **[measured]** `MeshWorkerCore.test.ts`, through the job the pool actually
> posts: a patch of placed blocks changes the geometry at lod 0, 1, 2 **and** 3.
> Break either half of the fix and the test fails.

### What still closes up at distance, by design

A coarse cell holds `4 ^ lod` fine cells and reads as air only when **every**
change inside it was a break. So a one-block hole in a hillside closes up as you
walk away, and a wall stays a wall — that is doc 14's rule and it is what stops
distant terrain dissolving into pinholes. A **placed** block survives to every
level, which is why the test places rather than digs.

### One more thing it turned up

`ChunkDeltas.set` packed a slot and a layer into one word without checking
either. A layer of `-1` packs as every bit set and reads back as slot 2,097,151
of layer 2,047 — a cell no chunk has, silently corrupt on disk, and thrown far
away from whatever wrote it. It refuses now, where the mistake is made.
