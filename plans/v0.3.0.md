# v0.3.0

Minor: the maps. What a planet is made of, beyond a height.

Worked through in the three steps
[`HOW-TO-WRITE-PLANS.md`](../HOW-TO-WRITE-PLANS.md) sets out — discuss, try,
implement. Steps 1 and 2 run per item; step 3 runs once, for the whole release,
and starts when every item in the scope has cleared step 2.

**The release is about where the land goes and what stands on it.** The coarse
map decides the coastline by cutting a noise field at the height that leaves the
intended fraction above, and a cut through a smooth field draws a smooth curve.
Under that coastline there is one tier and no shelf, and beside the height there
is nothing — no temperature, no humidity, no biome.

**It is judged in [`v0.2.0`](v0.2.0.md)'s editor**, which is why that release
came first. Three of the items below are settled by looking at a map rather than
by reading a number, and the editor is what makes looking possible.

**Every existing seed produces a different world.** I-1 changes where the land
is. Nothing is saved yet, so nothing is lost.

## Items

### I-1 — The coastline is a threshold on noise, so the land comes out as blobs

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18 — reference images from a world editor whose
coastlines are ragged and strewn with islands, against ours which are not
**Step:** 2, and **judged in the engine** — all four go in behind a switch, the
editor draws them, the losers come out in this release

#### The problem

`continentHeight` sums two tiers of `fbm` and `seaLevelFor` cuts the result at
the percentile that leaves the intended land fraction standing. A percentile cut
through a smooth field draws a smooth curve. The coasts come out rounded,
islands are rare and roughly circular, and there is no archipelago anywhere on
the planet.

#### Candidates

**A — What ships.** Kept as a candidate rather than as a baseline, because the
trial found it inside the range real coasts occupy rather than outside it.

**B — Warp the direction before the continent lookup.** Sample a second `fbm`
field and use it to displace the unit direction, then evaluate the continent
tier at the displaced point. A smooth field pushed around by another smooth
field has a folded level set, so the same percentile cut draws a ragged curve.
Two parameters: how far the warp pushes, and at what feature size.

**C — Grow the land level by level on the lattice.** Hold a binary land or sea
state per cell and build it up the subdivision hierarchy. At the coarsest level
every cell is land with a hashed probability (**creation**), and a few isolated
cells are seeded away from the rest (**island**). Refining to the next level,
each child inherits its parent's state and then flips with a probability that
depends on how many of its neighbours hold the other state (**growth weight**).
Randomness is hashed from the seed and the cell's coordinates, never drawn from
a running generator, so the result does not depend on the order cells are
visited.

**D — Put the land on plates.** Scatter a few dozen seeds and give every cell
its nearest, which is a Voronoi diagram on the sphere and the same `argmax` of
dot products doc 04 uses for faces. Give each plate an angular velocity — a
hashed axis and rate, so a plate's motion at a cell is the cross product of that
axis with the cell — and a hashed bias that makes it oceanic or continental.
Where two plates close on each other along the step between two cells, raise a
range; where they part, drop a rift. Carry that inland, weaker at every step.

Every direction is hashed and normalised rather than built from an angle. A
plate laid out with `sin` and `cos` would put a transcendental in a field two
clients have to agree on to the bit; three hashed components and one `normalize`
are a wrapping multiply and a square root, both pinned by IEEE 754.

[Doc 21](../docs/21-rivers-and-erosion.md) has recommended plates since before
the engine existed. It is the only one of the four that puts a mountain range
somewhere for a reason.

**Conway's rule itself is not a candidate.** Its birth and survival counts are
written for an eight-neighbour square neighbourhood; on six neighbours, five at
the twelve pentagons, every threshold has to be re-derived. What the reference
images show is the growth-and-refine loop rather than the rule, and C carries
that without inheriting a numbering that does not fit the grid.

#### Trial

`verification/coastline.js`. One seed, land fraction 0.3, levels 5 to 8.

**How ragged, as a rate rather than a shape.** Count the cell edges along the
largest landmass's coast, halve the cells, count again. A curve carrying no
detail below the map's resolution doubles exactly. The excess over 2 is what
ragged means as a number, and it does not depend on which level it is read at.

| | level 5 | 6 | 7 | growth | dimension |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A, today | 781 | 1,623 | 3,523 | `x2.08` `x2.17` | **1.06 – 1.12** |
| B, warped | 827 | 1,865 | 4,087 | `x2.26` `x2.19` | **1.13 – 1.17** |
| C, grown | 1,007 | 2,655 | 7,239 | `x2.64` `x2.73` | **1.40 – 1.45** |
| D, plates | 993 | 2,807 | 6,373 | `x2.83` `x2.27` | **1.18 – 1.50** |

Real coasts, quoted and not measured here, run from about 1.05 for South Africa
through 1.25 for Britain to about 1.52 for Norway. **What ships is at the smooth
end of the real range rather than outside it**, which is a milder verdict than
the item assumed.

**What each does to the land**, at level 7:

| | land | largest landmass | islands | longest river |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A, today | 30.0% | 27,305 | 45 | **172** |
| B, warped | 30.0% | 26,913 | 43 | 153 |
| C, grown | 30.4% | 14,910 | 312 | 83 |
| D, plates | 30.0% | 25,072 | 136 | 94 |

**The two that build structure both charge for it, in different places.** C
gives up the land fraction as a number that can be asked for — there is no
percentile in it, so `creation` was searched by bisection and arrives near 30%
rather than on it — and halves the largest landmass. D keeps both, and loses the
river length a different way: a range along every seam cuts the interior into
separate basins, so a river runs from a ridge to the nearest coast instead of
across the continent. The land is not what limits it there; the ground is.

**Preview fidelity settles nothing, because the editor draws at full
resolution.** Measured anyway, level 6 against level 8: A disagrees with itself
on 7 cells of 40,962, B on 4, C on 901, D on 1,528. What it still says is what
happens when somebody changes the coarse spacing — a noise field keeps its
coastline and the other two redraw it.

**B is close to a no-op at the one setting tried** — dimension 1.13 to 1.17
against 1.06 to 1.12, which is not a change anyone would see. Only `0.35`
amplitude at frequency `1.6` was run, and the editor is where the amplitude gets
swept.

**What the trial got wrong on the way.** The plate uplift first spread the seam
value from the cell it was reached from, which is a seam cell only on the first
step and carries zero afterwards, so every range came out two cells wide
whatever the range was set to. That made the ranges a level-dependent width in
metres, and it read as a property of plates until it was measured.

#### Why this is judged by eye

The measurements rule out nothing. They price four planets and the choice
between them is what a person wants a world to look like, which is
[`HOW-TO-WRITE-PLANS.md`](../HOW-TO-WRITE-PLANS.md)'s case for putting every
side in behind a switch. **F-019 applies**: the close may be "confirmed as a
per-planet knob" rather than a winner, and a small calm world may want a
different answer from a large dramatic one.

### I-2 — Nothing knows how far it is from the sea

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18 — the banded map in the reference images
**Step:** 2

#### The goal

Give every cell one number: **how many metres to the nearest coast**, negative
at sea and positive on land.

Three things read that one number, which is why it is a field and not three
separate calculations.

- **The baseline height.** Ground that falls away as it leaves the shore and
  rises as it goes inland, before any noise is added. Today there is no such
  tier — the ground goes straight from summed noise to a sea level, so a coast
  has no shelf and an interior has no rise.
- **Humidity**, in I-4. How far you are from water is most of what humidity is.
- **Coastal materials**, later. A beach, a shelf and a salt flat are each a band
  at a distance.

The banded map in the reference images is this field drawn with a repeating
palette, which is why its contours follow every inlet.

#### Candidates

**A — Count rings.** Start at every cell that touches a cell of the other kind.
Step outward one ring at a time, writing 1, 2, 3, and so on. Multiply by the
cell spacing at the end to get metres.

**B — Add up real distances.** Start in the same places, but each step adds the
actual metres between the two cells, taken off a sorted queue. `MinHeap` is
already here for `fillPits`.

The difference is that **cells are not all the same size**: spacing varies
`1.41:1` across the sphere. So counting rings measures a slightly different
distance in different parts of the planet, and A's bands may come out wider at a
face centre than at a face corner.

#### Trial, so far

Ring counting is built, in `verification/coastline.js` section 3, and it
**reaches every cell**: on the level-7 grown mask the field runs from 47 cells
at the deepest sea to 26 cells inland, with none left unvisited. One pass, no
queue.

The profile was run with breakpoints at `-60`, `-6`, `-1`, `1`, `40` and `200`
cells, and it does something the item did not predict. **It tempers the coast
rather than inheriting it.** Laying relief over the baseline and re-cutting at
sea level pulls the thinnest filaments back under: the grown mask's ratio falls
from `36.78` to `28.61` and its islands from 312 to 193.

That matters for I-1 as well as here — the coastline a player sees is the one
after the profile, not the mask.

**What is left**: whether counting rings bands unevenly, which needs the two
distance fields drawn side by side rather than counted, and is what the editor
is for.

#### The profile

The baseline height is a lookup. Hand it a distance, it hands back metres above
or below sea level. Two ways to write that lookup.

**A — Named points, straight lines between them.** "At the coast, 0 m. Four
hundred metres out to sea, −60 m. Three kilometres inland, +80 m." Every number
is a place a person can find on the map and point at.

**B — One curve, two numbers.** How high the interior gets, and how quickly it
gets there. Fewer numbers, and neither of them is anywhere in particular.

**A is the one to try first.** A breakpoint stated in metres is a knob whose
effect can be seen, and four of them rather than two is not what makes a panel
hard to use.

### I-3 — Every field costs four bytes whether it needs them or not

**From:** the map stack growing, 2026-08-18
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

`CoarseMap` holds four `Float32Array` fields. This release adds at least six
more: the land mask, border distance, baseline height, temperature, humidity,
biome noise and biome. **Only four of them need a float.** A biome is a byte, a
mask is a bit, a step count is two bytes.

At level 8's 655,362 cells:

| Field | Width | Bytes |
|---|---|---|
| height, water, flow, slope, baseline | `float32` | 2.5 MB each |
| border distance | `int16` | 1.3 MB |
| land mask | `uint8`, or packed bits at 82 KB | 0.65 MB |
| temperature, humidity, biome noise, biome | `uint8` | 0.65 MB each |

Eleven fields at their own widths is about **16 MB**. The same eleven at
`float32` is **27 MB**. The difference is one more level of map, which is what
actually buys landscape.

**v0.2.0's I-2 already decided how a field is described** for drawing. This item
is the storage half only.

#### Candidates

**A — A width per field, on the existing shape.** Each field keeps its property
and gains the array type that fits it. Types hold end to end, and every reader
knows what it is holding. What it costs: a reader that wants a normalised value
has to know a `uint8` field spans 0 to 255, and nothing enforces it.

**B — A width per field, behind a reader.** The same arrays, reached through a
call that returns a number in a stated range whatever the array underneath is.
One rule for every field. What it costs: a call where there is an array index
today, on a path that runs per cell.

**The measurement is bytes and the per-cell cost**: total resident bytes at
levels 8 and 9, and the time to read a whole field under each candidate, against
the direct array read.

### I-4 — A planet has height and nothing else about it

**From:** the owner, 2026-08-18
**Step:** 1

#### The goal

Four more fields, generated, documented, verified and demonstrated, and read by
nothing:

| Field | Holds |
|---|---|
| temperature | how warm a cell is |
| humidity | how wet |
| biome noise | a decorrelating field, so biome edges are not perfectly zonal |
| biome | one index per cell, from the other three and the elevation |

**Nothing consumes them in this release.** They are built so the model can be
argued about against a picture rather than in the abstract, and so the field
stack has more than one shape of field in it before I-3's decision is locked.

The axis is not a choice: [doc 20](../docs/20-player-coordinates.md) fixes the
poles at icosahedron vertices 0 and 3 and the prime meridian at vertex 11, so
latitude is already defined and every pentagon sits at a multiple of 36° of
longitude.

#### Bands at real latitudes are a requirement, not a candidate

The dry places go where they go on a real planet: wet at the equator, dry at the
horse latitudes, wet again in the temperate band, dry at the poles. A model that
scatters deserts wherever its noise happens to fall does not meet that, so it is
not a candidate — it is a model that fails the requirement.

Temperature is settled by the same sentence. It falls from equator to pole and
falls again with elevation at a stated lapse rate, plus a little noise. Three
numbers a person can picture: equator temperature, pole temperature, lapse rate.
Both candidates below share it and neither changes it.

What is left open is **how humidity gets its bands**, and the two ways differ in
what they give beyond the bands.

#### Candidates

**A — Read humidity off the latitude.** A curve of humidity against latitude
with the four bands written into it, plus I-2's distance to water, plus noise.
One lookup per cell. Every planet gets the same bands in the same places, and a
desert is a latitude.

**B — Carry the moisture.** Start with moisture over water, move it along
[doc 32](../docs/32-sky-clouds-and-moon.md)'s single wind axis for a fixed
number of steps, and drop it where the ground rises. The bands still appear,
because evaporation and the wind both follow latitude — but they arrive as a
result rather than as a table, and two things come with them that A cannot
produce: **a dry shadow behind a mountain range**, and **a continental interior
that dries with distance from the coast**.

What it costs: an iterative pass over the grid rather than a lookup, a number of
steps nobody can set by looking at one cell, and it makes doc 32's wind axis a
climate input when it was pinned for clouds.

#### What decides it

Both have to band, so banding is the gate rather than the measurement: the
latitude distribution of the driest cells, which should show four bands per
hemisphere either way.

The measurement that separates them is **whether a desert is ever behind a
mountain**. Count dry cells downwind of high ground against dry cells upwind of
it. A gives no difference at all, by construction. If B's difference is small on
this planet's relief, A is the model and the extra pass buys a rain shadow
nobody can see.

Against both, the fraction of land in each biome, against a stated target.

### I-5 — Erosion is measured in grid units

**From:** F-017, promoted, 2026-08-18
**Findings pulled in:** F-017
**Step:** 1

#### The problem

`erode` lowers a cell by `rate * sqrt(flow) * drop`, where `flow` counts
upstream cells and `drop` is a height difference between neighbours. Neither is
in metres. At a finer level `flow` is four times larger for the same ground and
`drop` is about half, so the errors mostly cancel — and only mostly. Measured
over five large valleys on the 6,800 m planet, floor-to-rim depth came out at
**35.4 m at a 32 m coarse cell against 34.4 m at 16 m** once the relief tier was
stated in metres, and at **11–37 m against 20–43 m** before it was.

`erosionRate` is therefore a knob that means a different amount of cutting on
every planet size and every coarse spacing.

**Its urgency went when the editor did.** The item was first written as a
dependency of a preview built at a reduced level, which would have eroded
differently from the map it previewed. v0.2.0's editor draws at full resolution,
so preview and Apply run the same numbers and the two cannot disagree. What is
left is F-017's own reason: a knob whose meaning moves with the planet.

#### Candidates

**A — Give `erode` the planet.** Divide `drop` by the coarse spacing to get a
gradient in metres per metre, and multiply `flow` by the cell area to get a
catchment in square metres. `erode` has to know the radius, which the coarse map
deliberately does not.

**B — Normalise by level in `buildCoarseMap`.** Scale `rate` by the level before
it reaches `erode`, where the level is already known and the radius is not
needed. `erode` stays a function of a grid.

A is stated in the units the result has and needs the coarse map to learn the
radius. B keeps the boundary where it is and leaves the incision written in grid
units with a correction in front of it.

**The measurement is F-017's**: valley depth on one seed at 32 m and 16 m,
before and after. It should stop moving with resolution.
