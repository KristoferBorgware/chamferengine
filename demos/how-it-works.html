Chamfer · A walkthrough

How the grid is built

The whole design rests on one geometric trick, applied four times. Once you can see it, the addressing, the meshing and the level-of-detail scheme all follow from it — and none of them need any more cleverness.

What we are trying to achieve

A planet made of blocks, like Minecraft, but round. You can walk in any direction forever and come back to where you started, and nowhere on the way does the ground go strange — no stretched blocks, no seam, no pole.

Everything below is in service of that one sentence.

1Start with a shape that is nearly a ball

A sphere cannot be tiled evenly by any regular shape, so we start with the closest thing that is even: an icosahedron — twenty identical triangles arranged into a ball-ish solid. Twenty is the most faces any perfectly regular solid can have, which makes it the most even starting point that exists.

It is lumpy. That is fine. Everything from here is about making it less lumpy without ever making it uneven.

2Split every triangle into four, over and over

Take one triangle. Mark the middle of each of its three edges. Join those three marks. You now have four smaller triangles inside the original, and they fit it exactly — no gaps, no overlap, nothing left over.

Do that to all twenty faces, then do it again to every triangle you just made, and again. The count goes 20 → 80 → 320 → 1,280, multiplying by four every time. Each round is called a level.

The thing to notice: a small triangle is always completely inside exactly one bigger triangle. Nothing straddles a boundary, ever, at any level. That single property is what later makes chunks, streaming and level-of-detail all work without special cases.

3Push the corners out onto the ball

Splitting a flat triangle keeps it flat. So after each split, every corner point is pushed straight outward from the centre until it sits exactly on the sphere's surface.

This is one line of arithmetic — normalize(position), meaning "keep the direction, set the length to the radius". The solid gets rounder every level. By level 5 or 6 it looks like a ball; by level 11 you could not tell it from one.

4The blocks are the corners, not the triangles

Here is the trick the whole design turns on, and it is the one thing people expect to be the other way round.

You might assume each little triangle is a block. It isn't. Each corner point is a block. The triangles are only scaffolding — a filing system for finding things. What the player walks on is the territory around each corner point, and that territory is a hexagon.

Why is that territory a hexagon? Because six triangles meet at every interior corner. Walk around a corner point and you pass through six triangles, so the patch of ground nearer to it than to any neighbour has six sides.

This is the payoff. Hexagons never fit inside bigger hexagons — that is a real mathematical obstacle, and it is why other hex grid systems (like Uber's H3) only get approximate nesting. But triangles nest perfectly. So we put the filing system on the triangles, and the gameplay on the hexagons, and neither has to compromise.

Where the twelve pentagons come from

At the twelve original corners of the icosahedron, only five triangles meet instead of six. So those twelve cells have five sides, not six. No amount of subdividing changes this — split forever and there are still exactly twelve, in exactly those places.

Chunks are just triangles, and the address is the route

Because triangles nest exactly, a chunk — the piece of world that gets loaded, saved and drawn as a unit — is simply "one triangle, at whatever level you choose to cut". Nothing else is needed.

And a cell's address is literally the route you took to reach it: which of the 20 faces, then which quarter, then which quarter of that, and so on. Each step is one digit from 0 to 3.

Turning one cell into triangles

Graphics cards only draw triangles. So every hexagon has to become triangles before it can appear on screen. A hexagon needs four: pick one corner, and fan out to the non-adjacent ones.

A block is not flat, though — it is a hexagonal prism, a hexagon with depth, running down toward the planet's core. It has a top, a bottom, and six sides: eight faces to a cube's six.

Turning a chunk into triangles

A chunk is one triangle's worth of surface, all the way down. Meshing it is three passes:

  • Tops. For every cell whose ceiling is air, emit 4 triangles.
  • Sides. For each of the 6 directions, walk down the column and merge every unbroken run of exposed faces into one rectangle.
  • Skirt. Around the chunk's rim, hang a short vertical apron downward — the reason is on the next diagram.

Sideways merging, on the other hand, does not work. In a hex grid, the same-facing sides of neighbouring cells are parallel but staggered — they zigzag instead of lining up, so there is no straight run to collapse. That is the piece of cube-world meshing that genuinely does not transfer.

Level of detail, and why it is resampling

Far-away ground does not need full resolution. In a cube world you handle this by dropping every other block. Here you cannot — because hexagons at one level are not made of hexagons from the next, the coarse grid is a genuinely different set of cells, not a subset.

The level to use is chosen by altitude, not by distance. On a 1,700 m planet the ground curves away only 76 m in front of a standing player, so at eye height the entire visible world is about 21,000 cells — nothing at all. Climb, and it grows fast:

Which is why the seams need skirts

When two neighbouring chunks pick different levels, they sample the terrain at different spacings, so their edges land at slightly different heights. That leaves a crack you can see straight through to the sky.

One last limit: you cannot flatten too much at once

It is tempting to merge a big field of identical blocks into one huge flat polygon. On a flat world that is free. On a ball it is not: a flat patch cuts across the curve, so the middle of the patch sinks below where the ground should actually be.

The whole thing in one breath

  • Start with 20 triangles. Split each into 4, repeatedly. Push corners onto the ball.
  • The corners are the blocks; each one owns a hexagon. Triangles are only the filing system.
  • Twelve corners have five neighbours instead of six. Those are the twelve pentagons, and they are unavoidable.
  • An address is the route down the splits, so chopping digits off gives you the containing chunk for free.
  • A cell costs 2 vertices and 4 triangles. Sides merge vertically for free; nothing merges sideways.
  • Detail level follows altitude. Coarse meshes re-ask the terrain function rather than dropping cells, and skirts hide the seams.
